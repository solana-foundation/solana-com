<Callout type="info" title="Summary">

Confidential balances encrypt amounts. They do not hide who transacted with
whom, or when. Account-level controls - freeze, default-frozen, pause, permanent
delegate - are unaffected, because they never depended on amounts. Any policy
that depends on the size of a transfer cannot be enforced onchain once amounts
are encrypted. Decide which of those you need before you create the mint,
because the extension cannot be added later.

</Callout>

## What is hidden and what is not

The
[confidential transfer extension](/docs/tokens/extensions/confidential-transfer)
encrypts transfer amounts and account balances. Everything else about a transfer
stays public.

| Public                                 | Encrypted        |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| Sender and recipient account addresses | Transfer amounts |
| Account owners                         | Account balances |
| The mint                               |                  |
| That a transfer occurred, and when     |                  |

So an observer cannot see how much moved, but can see that these two parties
transacted, at this time, in your asset. **The transaction graph is intact.**

This distinction decides whether the extension solves your problem. If the
concern is commercially sensitive position sizes or payment amounts, it solves
it. If the concern is that a competitor can see your client list, or that
counterparty relationships are themselves confidential, it does not - and no
amount of encryption at the balance layer will, because the addresses are the
graph. That is a topology question, covered in the
[deployment decision guide](/docs/institutional/architecture/deployment-decision-guide).

## Which controls still work

The split is between controls that act on _accounts_ and controls that act on
_amounts_.

| Control                                   | Works with confidential balances                                      |
| ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Default account state (frozen by default) | Yes - operates on the account, not its balance                        |
| Freeze and thaw, including via Token ACL  | Yes - the gate decides who may hold, which is orthogonal to how much  |
| Pausable                                  | Yes - halts activity token-wide                                       |
| Permanent delegate                        | Yes - the authority is over the account, not conditioned on an amount |
| Permissioned burn                         | Yes                                                                   |
| Attestation-driven allowlisting           | Yes - the claim is about the holder                                   |
| Any rule conditioned on transfer size     | **No** - see below                                                    |

The pattern is that the whole frozen-by-default plus Token ACL design, which is
the recommended starting point in
[choosing controls](/docs/institutional/token-design/choosing-controls), is
compatible with confidential balances. Who may hold the token and how much they
move are independent questions.

### Amount-dependent policy

An onchain program cannot evaluate a value it cannot read. Once amounts are
encrypted, no program in the transfer path can enforce a per-transfer cap, a
daily volume limit, a threshold that triggers extra approval, or a
jurisdiction-dependent transfer size. This follows from the extension's design
rather than from an incomplete implementation: no instruction exposes the amount
to a program in the transfer path. Treat it as a fixed constraint for planning
purposes.

If you need those rules, you have three options: drop confidential balances,
move the rule off-chain to a point where you can see the amount (your own
issuance and redemption path, which you control), or accept the rule as a
contractual term enforced by audit rather than by the ledger.

<Callout type="warn">

Before committing to a design that combines confidential balances with a
transfer hook or any other extension, confirm the extensions can be co-enabled
at all. Some Token-2022 extensions are mutually exclusive - see
[Token Extensions](/docs/tokens/extensions). Treat the amount-visibility
constraint above as certain, and the co-enablement question as one to verify
against the program for your specific combination.

</Callout>

## Decisions fixed at mint creation

The confidential transfer extension must be initialized **before the mint is
initialized and cannot be added later**. If there is a realistic chance you will
want confidentiality, enabling the extension at creation keeps the option open.

Two settings are chosen at creation, though both can be changed afterwards:

- **Approve policy.** Whether accounts may opt into confidential transfers
  permissionlessly, or must be approved by the mint's confidential transfer
  authority. Manual approval gives you a second gate on who transacts
  confidentially; automatic approval removes an operational step.
- **Auditor key.** An optional global auditor ElGamal public key. When set, the
  holder of the corresponding secret key can decrypt every transfer amount for
  the mint.

Confidential transfer fees and confidential mint/burn are companion extensions
that must also be initialized at creation if you want them.

<Callout type="warn">

Confidential transfers depend on the ZK ElGamal Proof Program for onchain proof
verification, so the mint is only usable on clusters where that program is
enabled. Confirm this for your target cluster before you build against it.

</Callout>

## Auditor keys and disclosure

The auditor key is how a confidential token stays supervisable. Set it, and the
public sees no amounts while the auditor sees every one.

Three things about it matter operationally:

1. **The secret key is a standing decryption capability over the entire mint.**
   Custody it with the same care as the mint authority. Whoever holds it can
   read every transfer settled while it was the active auditor key.
2. **Rotation does not re-key history.** Past transfers stay encrypted to
   whichever auditor key was active when they executed. If you rotate, retain
   the old keys or you permanently lose the ability to decrypt historical
   activity - including for an examination that arrives years later. Build key
   retention into your records schedule.
3. **Clearing the auditor is not neutral.** Setting the auditor to none removes
   amount visibility for everyone except the account owners themselves. That may
   be the right answer, but document the decision rather than leaving the
   auditor unset by default.

For disclosure narrower than "the auditor sees everything," you rely on the
account owner: a holder can demonstrate their own balance and transfers to a
counterparty or supervisor. Design your processes so a routine query goes to the
holder, and the auditor key is reserved for cases where you cannot rely on their
cooperation.

## Choosing

- **You need amount privacy and account-level controls.** Confidential balances
  plus frozen-by-default and Token ACL. This composes cleanly.
- **You need amount privacy and amount-dependent rules.** These conflict. Move
  the rule off-chain or drop the privacy requirement - decide which, explicitly.
- **You need the counterparty graph hidden.** Confidential balances are not the
  answer. See the
  [deployment decision guide](/docs/institutional/architecture/deployment-decision-guide).
- **You need none of the above.** Do not enable the extension. It adds
  integration work for holders and venues, and unused complexity is a diligence
  question you may have to answer.
