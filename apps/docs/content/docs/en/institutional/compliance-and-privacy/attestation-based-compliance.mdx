<Callout type="info" title="Summary">

Run the KYC, sanctions, and travel-rule checks off-chain exactly as you do
today. Publish only the _result_ onchain, as an attestation asserting a narrow
claim - "this address passed sanctions screening, valid until this date." The
token's gate reads the attestation. The evidence behind the claim never leaves
your systems.

</Callout>

## The problem this solves

A gated token needs an onchain answer to "may this address hold the token?" The
inputs to that answer - identity documents, screening hits, beneficial-ownership
records - are personal data that must not go onchain. Solana's ledger is public
and permanent; there is no delete.

Attestations separate the two. The check runs where it always ran, inside your
compliance function or your vendor's. What lands onchain is a signed statement
that the check ran and what it concluded.

<Callout type="warn">

Attestation data is stored onchain and is public and permanent. The pattern
protects personal data only because you choose to attest a narrow conclusion.
Putting a name, a document number, a date of birth, a residential address, or a
raw screening payload into an attestation defeats the entire design and cannot
be undone. Attest booleans and coarse categories, never the inputs.

</Callout>

## The three objects

[Solana Attestations](/docs/tools/attestations) is built from three onchain
objects. Mapping them to who owns what in an issuance:

| Object          | What it is                                                    | Who owns it in practice                                                      |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Credential**  | An attestation authority, with a list of authorized signers   | The institution standing behind the check - you, or your KYC provider        |
| **Schema**      | The shape and field types a valid attestation must conform to | The issuer, so every attester makes the same claim in the same format        |
| **Attestation** | A signed claim about a subject, conforming to a schema        | Created by an authorized signer of the credential, one per address per claim |

An attestation carries an expiration timestamp and a unique nonce, and can
optionally be linked to a specific token account. See
[credentials](/docs/tools/attestations/credentials),
[schemas](/docs/tools/attestations/schemas), and
[attestations](/docs/tools/attestations/attestations) for the mechanics.

### Who signs

The credential's authorized-signer list is the compliance decision, not a
technical one. Two arrangements are common:

- **You attest.** Your compliance function holds the credential and signs. You
  are asserting your own conclusion, and you carry the responsibility for it.
- **Your provider attests.** Your KYC or screening vendor holds a credential and
  signs. You rely on their claim, which means your reliance arrangement with
  them now has an onchain expression, and you should be able to point at the
  contract that backs it.

Multiple credentials can coexist for one token - a sanctions attester and an
accreditation attester, for example, each with its own schema.

## Designing the schema

Schema design determines how much the pattern exposes. Keep every field to the
narrowest thing that supports the gate decision.

Reasonable fields:

- A boolean: screening passed, accreditation confirmed, eligibility met.
- A coarse jurisdiction code, where the rule genuinely varies by jurisdiction.
- A tier or category, where holding limits differ by investor class.
- A validity window, which the expiration timestamp already gives you.
- An opaque reference to your own case file, meaningful only inside your systems
  and not derivable from onchain data.

Fields to keep out: anything identifying a person, anything describing the
evidence, and anything precise enough to re-identify by combination. A birth
date plus a postcode is personal data even though neither field is a name.

Version the schema from the start. Rules change, and a schema you cannot
supersede without invalidating every existing attestation is a migration you may
end up running under pressure.

## Wiring an attestation to the gate

Two places the claim can be enforced, with different costs.

### Drive the allowlist

Your service watches for attestations, and when a valid one appears for an
address, it adds that address to the Token ACL allowlist. The holder then thaws
their own account against the list.

This is the default. Per-transfer cost is nothing, because by transfer time the
question is already settled - the account is thawed or it is not. The trade is
latency between the attestation and the list update, and an off-chain service
you have to keep running and monitored.

See [gate the holders](/docs/institutional/tutorials/issuing/gate-the-holders)
for the allowlist mechanics.

### Check inside a transfer hook

A [transfer hook](/docs/tokens/extensions/transfer-hook) reads the attestation
account at transfer time and rejects the transfer if it is missing, expired, or
revoked.

This gives you enforcement with no lag - a revocation bites on the very next
transfer - at the cost of compute on every transfer, extra accounts the sender
must supply, and reduced composability. Choose it when the freshness of the
claim is itself the requirement.

<Callout type="warn">

If your mint uses confidential balances, a hook cannot see transfer amounts. Any
rule that depends on the size of a transfer cannot be enforced this way. See
[privacy and transfer controls](/docs/institutional/compliance-and-privacy/privacy-and-transfer-controls).

</Callout>

## Lifecycle

The attestation is a snapshot of a check, and checks go stale. Decide these
before you launch:

- **Expiry.** Set an expiration that matches your re-screening cycle. An
  attestation with no meaningful expiry is a claim you are making indefinitely.
- **Re-attestation.** Who re-runs the check, on what schedule, and does the
  holder have to do anything? Prefer renewal that completes without holder
  action over a lapse discovered at transfer time.
- **Revocation.** A sanctions hit against an existing holder is the case to plan
  for. Under the allowlist pattern, removing the address stops new thaws but
  **does not freeze an already-thawed account** - you must freeze it explicitly.
  Under the hook pattern, the next transfer fails on its own. Know which
  behavior you have.
- **A holder whose attestation lapses while holding a balance.** They still hold
  the asset. Decide in advance whether that means frozen, transfer-only-to-
  redeem, or a grace period, and write it into the token's terms rather than
  discovering it in an incident.

## What this does not give you

- **It is not proof the check was done well.** An attestation is a signature on
  a claim. Its value is exactly the standing of whoever signed it, so the
  standing of the attester is what an assessment turns on.
- **It does not carry travel-rule data.** The travel rule requires originator
  and beneficiary information to move between institutions. That is personal
  data and stays off-chain, on your existing rails. What an attestation can do
  is assert that the counterparty is an institution you have a travel-rule
  arrangement with - that is, the assurance rather than the underlying data.
- **It does not hide who holds the token.** Addresses, balances, and the fact
  that an address carries an attestation are all public. If the holder graph
  itself is sensitive, that is a separate problem - see
  [privacy and transfer controls](/docs/institutional/compliance-and-privacy/privacy-and-transfer-controls).
- **It does not resolve identity across chains or institutions.** An attestation
  is about an address, not a person. Two addresses attested by the same provider
  are not linked by the attestations themselves, though issuance patterns may
  still support inference - and a case-file reference reused across both would
  link them directly.
