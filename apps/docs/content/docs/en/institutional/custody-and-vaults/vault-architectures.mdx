<Callout type="info" title="Summary">

A vault is a program that holds assets and issues claims against them. The
central design question is whether a deposit or withdrawal can complete in the
same transaction. If the asset has a price that strikes periodically rather than
continuously, it cannot, and you need an async request-and-settle design. That
covers most institutional fund structures.

</Callout>

## Synchronous or async

**A synchronous vault** settles a deposit or withdrawal in one transaction. The
depositor sends assets and receives shares immediately, priced from state the
program can read at that moment.

This works when the price is knowable onchain at transaction time - a vault over
liquid onchain assets with a reliable oracle. It does not work when the price is
struck off-chain, periodically, by an administrator.

**An async vault** splits the operation in two. The participant submits a
_request_; later, at a valuation point, the operator _settles_ it at the struck
price. Between the two, the participant holds a claim, not shares.

This is the shape institutional funds need, because it matches how they already
work: subscriptions and redemptions accepted against a cut-off, priced at the
next valuation point, settled after. See
[NAV strikes](/docs/tokenization/nav-strikes) for a worked implementation of
this pattern for money market funds, including the delegated authority pattern
and the subscription and redemption flows.

## The async lifecycle

Four stages, and each has a decision attached.

1. **Request.** A participant commits assets - or shares, for a redemption - and
   receives a claim to be settled at the next valuation point. _Decide:_ are the
   committed assets escrowed by the program, or merely pledged? Escrow is
   simpler to reason about and reduces failure modes at settlement.
2. **Cut-off.** Requests after a point in time roll to the following valuation.
   _Decide:_ how the cut-off is enforced. A cut-off enforced only off-chain is
   not a cut-off if participants can still submit requests onchain.
3. **Strike.** The valuation is determined and published. _Decide:_ who may
   publish it, and what constrains them. This is among the most sensitive
   authorities in the design - it sets the price at which everyone's request
   settles.
4. **Settle.** Requests are executed at the struck price; shares are minted or
   burned and assets move. _Decide:_ what happens to a request that cannot
   settle - insufficient liquidity for a redemption, or a participant who has
   become ineligible between request and settlement.

That last case is the one to design deliberately. A participant can be
allowlisted when they subscribe and removed before settlement. The vault must
have a defined behavior - reject and return, hold pending, or settle to a
restricted state - rather than failing in a way that strands the assets.

## A reference implementation

The Solana Foundation publishes
[Vault Standard Suite](https://github.com/solana-foundation/vault), an
open-source Anchor program implementing the async shape described above.
Requests are queued and settled by a vault authority once a valuation is
published; shares and assets do not move atomically at request time.

The extension modules are opt-in: subscription and redemption queues, pausable
flows, fees, and minimum subscription and redemption sizes. The share mint is
supplied by the issuer rather than created by the program, and the program
composes with Token-2022 and sRFC-37 access control lists, which is what makes
it usable over a gated token.

Check the extension surface yourself. The program rejects an asset mint carrying
a nonzero transfer fee, but it does not reject a permanent delegate, a mint
close authority, or a freeze authority - the controls this section has spent
several pages recommending. Vetting those against your vault's assumptions is
left to you.

The repository carries TypeScript and Rust clients generated from the IDL,
integration tests, and design documentation including sequence diagrams for the
deposit, redeem and authority-withdraw flows.

<Callout type="warn" title="Not production-ready">

The program has had a security review from Cantina but is not audited for
mainnet use, and is deployed only on devnet. Treat it as a reference to read and
adapt, and run your own review before deploying anything derived from it.

</Callout>

## Interaction with the token's controls

A vault over a gated token is subject to that gate like any other holder:

- **The vault's own token account must be allowlisted.** It is an address
  holding the token; the gate does not exempt programs. Allowlist the vault's
  account as part of deployment, not after the first failed deposit.
- **Frozen-by-default applies to the vault too.** Its account is created frozen
  and must be thawed against the gate before it can receive anything.
- **A pause halts the vault.** If you pause the token, in-flight requests cannot
  settle. Decide whether that means they queue or fail, and make sure the
  incident-response runbook that authorizes a pause knows a vault is downstream.
- **The permanent delegate can move assets out of the vault's account.** This is
  expected behavior and occasionally necessary, but the vault's accounting must
  allow for it.
- **Share tokens are usually gated too.** If the fund's shares are themselves a
  restricted token, the participant's share account needs the same treatment as
  the vault's asset account, and both gates have to be satisfied for a
  subscription to complete.

Enumerate every account the vault touches and confirm each one's gate status
before deployment. See
[choosing controls](/docs/institutional/token-design/choosing-controls) for the
control surface these interactions come from.

## Settlement against cash

A subscription is an exchange of cash for shares and a redemption is the
reverse, so both are the delivery-versus-payment problem in
[payments and interoperability](/docs/institutional/payments-and-interoperability).
Where settlement happens in a single transaction, the exchange is atomic, so
neither side can deliver without receiving. A transaction that fails to land is
still a settlement exposure. [Delivery versus payment](/docs/tokenization/dvp)
covers the mechanics.

The async shape complicates this: the request and the settlement are separate
transactions, so atomicity applies to the settlement leg only. The exposure
between request and settlement is a real position that has to be measured and
governed.

## Operational questions to close

- Who holds the authority to publish a valuation, and what governs its use?
- What happens to an unsettleable request, in each failure case?
- How is the vault's own eligibility maintained if the allowlist changes?
- What is the recovery procedure if settlement fails partway through a batch?
- How does the vault's position reconcile against the register of record - see
  [transfer agent responsibilities](/docs/institutional/custody-and-vaults/transfer-agent-responsibilities).
