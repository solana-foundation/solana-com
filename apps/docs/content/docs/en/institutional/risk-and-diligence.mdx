<Callout type="info" title="Summary">

Each section below states a concern in the terms a reviewer might raise it, sets
out what is known to be true, describes where it applies, and names what would
change the answer. Where no mitigation exists, the section says so.

</Callout>

## How to read this page

Every section has the same four parts.

**The concern** states the question. **What is true** sets out what can be
stated accurately. **Where it applies** describes the cases it covers, and says
so where no mechanism exists. **What would change the answer** gives the
condition that would make the current assessment out of date, so it can be
revisited.

The topics here are the ones this documentation currently covers. They are not a
complete diligence checklist, and they are not a substitute for your own
assessment.

Network figures cited here change. Verify current values against the live
sources in [resources](/docs/institutional/resources) before using any of them
in a submission.

## Liveness and network halts

**The concern.** The network has halted before. An asset register that stops
accepting writes is an operational incident with regulatory consequences.

**What is true.** Solana has experienced full network halts, most recently in
February 2024, when a defect in the just-in-time program cache stopped block
production for approximately five hours. Earlier halts occurred between 2020
and 2024. Each was resolved by a coordinated validator restart, and each has a
published post-mortem.

**Where it applies.** These were liveness events. In each case the ledger
resumed from where it stopped and no finalized transaction was reversed.

Partial degradation is the more common event, and the network has continued to
produce and finalize blocks through it. The structural reason is that validator
operators concentrate in a small number of hosting providers and data centers,
so a single provider's outage, network incident, or policy change can take a
material share of stake offline at once without any protocol defect. Data-center
and provider distribution is published alongside the stake figures at
[validators.app](https://www.validators.app). A continuity plan should cover
degraded operation as well as total stoppage.

What a halt costs depends on where your register of record sits. Where the mint
is the register, a halt stops the register itself, and no transfer, mint, or
burn can be recorded until block production resumes. Where the token records an
asset held elsewhere, the off-chain register keeps operating and the chain falls
behind it, which makes a halt a reconciliation backlog rather than a stoppage.
The continuity plan differs accordingly - see
[token design decisions](/docs/institutional/token-design).

**What would change the answer.** A halt affecting a live institutional
register, or a liveness event that reached the threshold at which the network
stops finalizing. Track current status at
[status.solana.com](https://status.solana.com) and read the post-mortems
directly.

## Protocol finality versus legal finality

**The concern.** A transfer that is final under the protocol is not necessarily
final under the law of the jurisdiction where a liquidator will contest it.

**What is true.** These are different objects. The protocol property is
precisely defined and observable - see
[settlement and finality](/docs/institutional/settlement-finality). The legal
property depends on venue, governing law, the characterization of the token, and
in many jurisdictions on designation under a settlement finality regime. Each
regulatory regime treats this differently, and the treatment in one says little
about the treatment in another.

**Where it applies.** What can be narrowed is the scope: the uncertainty is
about legal recognition of the ledger, not about whether the ledger will
reorganize.

The exposure is largest where the mint is the register of record, because the
question is then whether the chain record is the definitive register. Where the
token records an asset held elsewhere, the off-chain register carries that
weight and the onchain question narrows to the transfer of a claim. The question
is narrower in that model, but it remains open.

**What would change the answer.** Jurisdictional recognition of a distributed
ledger record as the definitive register. Perimeters exist in several
jurisdictions.

## Transaction omission and censorship

**The concern.** A block producer chooses which transactions to include. One
could decline to include yours.

**What is true.** A leader has discretion over the contents of its own blocks
and can omit a transaction. Leaders are assigned in rotation, so a given leader
holds that discretion for a bounded number of consecutive slots.

**Where it applies.** Omission by one leader delays a transaction. The next
leader is a different operator, and the transaction can be included then, so
sustained exclusion would require coordination across a large share of stake.
The practical exposure is added latency, bounded by how long any single operator
holds block production.

**What would change the answer.** Concentration of block production such that a
small number of coordinating operators could exclude a transaction indefinitely.
This is the concentration question below, measured differently.

## Front-running and value extraction

**The concern.** Transactions are visible before they execute, and someone can
trade ahead of yours.

**What is true.** Ordering discretion within a block creates the opportunity to
insert transactions around a known one. The profit available from strategically
including, excluding, and reordering the transactions in a block is commonly
called maximal extractable value. Where a regime addresses it, it is generally
framed as a market integrity question, and regimes differ on how far that
framing reaches.

**Where it applies.** The exposure depends on the flow. A transfer between two
known parties at a pre-agreed price offers nothing for an observer to extract,
because there is no price for them to move. A trade executed against a public
liquidity pool does offer something. Most issuance and settlement flows fall
into the first category, and exposure arises mainly where a flow touches public
markets.

Where you do touch public liquidity, there are mitigations available - see
[MEV protection](/docs/defi/mev-protection) and
[stake-weighted quality of service](/docs/defi/stake-weighted-qos).

**What would change the answer.** Moving execution into venues where price
discovery happens onchain, which changes this from a low-relevance concern to a
primary one.

## Network fees and sanctioned recipients

**The concern.** Transaction fees are paid to whichever validator produces the
block. You cannot choose that validator, and you cannot rule out that it is
operated by a sanctioned person.

**What is true.** The issuer does not select the block producer, and validator
identities are pseudonymous by default. So a fee can in principle reach a
sanctioned or otherwise illicit operator without either party to the transaction
knowing. This is a recognized concern, and regulatory regimes differ in whether
and how they address it.

**Where it applies.** The mechanics bound the exposure. Half of the base fee is
burned rather than paid to anyone. The remaining half, plus the priority fee,
goes to the block producer. See [fee structure](/docs/core/fees/fee-structure)
for the current split and its source. The amounts are fractions of a cent per
transaction, paid to an unidentified counterparty as a protocol-level cost of
network access rather than as a commercial payment for services.

Whether this constitutes a sanctions exposure is a determination for your
compliance function and its counsel. This documentation sets out the mechanism,
the amounts involved, and the fact that neither party to a transaction selects
the recipient.

**What would change the answer.** A supervisory determination that
protocol-level fees to unidentified block producers constitute a prohibited
payment, or the emergence of a practical validator identity attestation that
lets fee flows be directed.

## Validator identity and operator concentration

**The concern.** The network depends on operators you have no relationship with,
whose identities you do not know and whose concentration you cannot control.

**What is true.** Both parts are accurate. Validator operators are pseudonymous
unless they choose otherwise, and stake is more concentrated than the raw
validator count suggests. The number of independent operators that would need to
collude to halt the network is published as the **Nakamoto coefficient**. Read
its current level and its direction of travel from the live source.

There is a second concentration that gets less attention. A large share of stake
runs client software from a single lineage. Agave and the clients built on it,
including Jito-Solana and Frankendancer, share its consensus and runtime code,
and therefore share its defects, so a count of distinct client names overstates
implementation diversity. Firedancer is the independently implemented client,
and the share of stake running it is the figure that speaks to this concern.

In August 2026, a routing failure at a hosting provider took roughly 29% of
staked SOL offline, approaching the threshold at which the network stops
finalizing blocks - but the chain kept producing blocks and confirming
transactions throughout. The fault originated outside Solana's consensus code,
reflecting validator infrastructure concentration rather than a protocol
weakness. Ongoing work continues to reduce this class of risk, including
validator client diversity through Firedancer and Agave alongside the Alpenglow
consensus upgrade, plus operator practices that spread stake across independent
hosting providers.

**Where it applies.** Concentration bears on whether the network keeps producing
blocks and on whose transactions are included. It does not bear on whether
balances are recorded correctly. Assess it with current figures.

**What would change the answer.** Meaningful stake migrating to an independently
implemented client, or a rising Nakamoto coefficient. Both are observable. Take
the figures from [validators.app](https://www.validators.app) rather than from
secondary summaries, and network activity and fee data from
[solana.com/data](https://solana.com/data).

## Upgrades and forks

**The concern.** The protocol changes without your consent, and a contentious
change could split the ledger your register depends on.

**What is true.** Protocol changes are proposed as improvement documents,
implemented behind feature gates, and activated on a schedule. You do not
control that schedule, and a change can alter behavior your systems depend on -
the ongoing reduction in slot time is a live example that invalidates any
hard-coded wall-clock assumption.

**Where it applies.** Changes are announced in advance, gated individually, and
observable onchain before they take effect. Feature gates are the authoritative
record of what is active; published status pages can lag the chain, so verify
against the gate. This places upgrades within the same change-management process
that applies to other infrastructure dependencies.

On forks specifically: a persistent split requires sustained disagreement across
a large share of stake. The relevant control for a register is that your own
systems record which fork an entry came from and can mark entries superseded.

**What would change the answer.** A contentious upgrade that produced a
persistent chain split. Track proposals in the improvement documents repository
and activation state in the feature gates.

## Privacy

**The concern.** Balances, counterparties, and transfer amounts are public. That
is incompatible with client confidentiality obligations and with commercial
sensitivity.

**What is true.** By default, a token account's balance and every transfer are
publicly readable. For many institutional use cases that is disqualifying on its
own.

**Where it applies.** Token-2022 provides
[confidential transfers](/docs/tokens/extensions/confidential-transfer), which
encrypt account balances and transfer amounts on the public ledger while keeping
them readable by keys the issuer designates.

The model is built for a supervised issuer. An issuer configures the mint with
the confidential transfer extension and nominates an auditor key. Holders
deposit from their public balance into a confidential balance, transfer
confidentially, and withdraw back to public when they need to. Each confidential
transfer carries a zero-knowledge proof that the arithmetic is correct, verified
onchain, so supply remains provably conserved even though the amounts are
encrypted. The auditor key decrypts amounts for the transfers it is designated
on, which gives selective disclosure to a supervisor or an internal audit
function without publishing anything.

The proof system these depend on is enabled on mainnet-beta, following a period
when it was disabled. Architectures that depend on confidential balances are
deployable. Two qualifications are worth stating in any assessment. First, the
scope is amounts and balances: account addresses and the fact that a transfer
occurred remain publicly visible, so the transaction graph is not concealed.
Second, adoption remains limited, including among issuers that have initialized
the extension without activating it for end users, so integration work may be
required that others have not yet done. See the
[issuer guide](/docs/tokens/extensions/confidential-transfer/issuer-guide) and
the
[integration guide](/docs/tokens/extensions/confidential-transfer/integration-guide).

**What would change the answer.** A requirement to conceal the transaction graph
itself, which confidential transfers do not address.

Work is underway in the ecosystem on this. The general approach is to hold
balances in encrypted form and prove transfers with zero-knowledge proofs, with
read-only viewing keys for selective disclosure, so that concealment from the
public ledger does not mean concealment from an auditor. Some designs also let
the rules for joining and transacting be declared onchain, so a participant can
inspect them before joining.

[Helius Privacy](https://www.helius.dev/docs/privacy/) is one illustrative
example of this category, named here because its documentation is public. It is
not an endorsement or a survey, and other projects are working in the same
space; assess any of them on your own criteria.

<Callout type="warn">

These are early-stage products. As of August 2026, Helius Privacy was in beta
and its documentation noted that names, endpoints, and behavior may change.
Treat graph-level privacy as a direction the ecosystem is moving in rather than
a capability to design a production system around, and re-assess when a given
product reaches general availability.

</Callout>

For the issuer-facing view of this - which controls still work once amounts are
encrypted, and how auditor keys and disclosure operate - see
[privacy and transfer controls](/docs/institutional/compliance-and-privacy/privacy-and-transfer-controls).
For where the asset should live if the transaction graph itself is the concern,
see the
[deployment decision guide](/docs/institutional/architecture/deployment-decision-guide).

## Identity, KYC, and legal certainty

**The concern.** You must know who holds your token, and an address is not an
identity.

**What is true.** An address carries no identity. The chain will not tell you
who controls one.

**Where it applies.** For an issuer, the relevant control is usually restricting
holding to addresses that have been onboarded. A frozen-by-default mint with an
allowlist provides that: the identity binding happens in the onboarding process,
and the chain enforces the resulting decision. See
[choosing controls](/docs/institutional/token-design/choosing-controls) and
[gate the holders](/docs/institutional/tutorials/issuing/gate-the-holders).

Onchain attestations can also bind a credential to an address without publishing
the underlying data - see [attestations](/docs/tools/attestations).

Travel rule obligations attach to the transfer between obliged entities and are
satisfied through the same channels as for other asset classes; the ledger
records the transfer, not the accompanying information.

**What would change the answer.** A requirement to identify counterparties
beyond your own holder set - for instance, an obligation extending to addresses
that merely interacted with your token.

## What this page does not cover yet

Five topics come up in reviews and are not documented here yet: singleness of
money across multiple settlement assets, interoperability and bridge risk,
quantum resilience, unsolicited token receipt and escheatment, and the effect of
ledger transparency on liquidity under stress. For several of them we have not
located specific standard-setter guidance, which is itself worth saying when the
question is raised.

To raise one of these ahead of it being documented, see the channels in
[resources](/docs/institutional/resources).
