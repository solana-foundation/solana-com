<Callout type="info" title="Summary">

For most regulated issuance, frozen-by-default accounts combined with Token ACL
are a reasonable starting point. The freeze-based design adds no per-transfer
compute and keeps the token usable in venues that do not execute custom code on
every transfer. A transfer hook suits rules that cannot be expressed as whether
an account is permitted to hold the token at all.

</Callout>

## Three ways to restrict who holds a token

Transfer restrictions are often implemented as a contract that runs on every
transfer. On Solana most of these controls are extensions enforced inside the
[Token-2022 program](/docs/tokens) itself, so they are shared standards rather
than bespoke code.

| Approach                          | How it works                                                                                        | Per-transfer cost         | Composability                                                              |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Frozen by default + Token ACL** | Every new token account is created frozen. A qualifying wallet thaws itself against an onchain list | None                      | High - the token is an ordinary Token-2022 mint to anyone reading it       |
| **Transfer hook**                 | A program you write is invoked on every transfer and can reject it                                  | Compute on every transfer | Lower - integrating venues must support hooks and account for your program |
| **Manual freeze and thaw**        | You hold the freeze authority and act on each account yourself                                      | None                      | High, but you are a liveness dependency for every new holder               |

### Why frozen-by-default suits most regulated issuance

[Default Account State](/docs/tokens/extensions/default-state) creates every
token account frozen. On its own this makes the issuer a bottleneck, since no
address can hold the token until it is thawed individually.

[Token ACL](/docs/tokenization/token-acl) removes that bottleneck. The freeze
authority is delegated to a shared program that exposes a permissionless thaw,
and that program delegates the allow-or-deny decision to a pluggable gate. With
an address-based list as the gate, a wallet on the allowlist thaws itself. The
issuer retains an override throughout: freeze or thaw directly, swap the gate,
or reclaim the freeze authority.

The result behaves like an ordinary Token-2022 mint to an integrator reading it,
while the holding restriction is enforced by the program.

<Callout type="warn">

Token ACL is specified in sRFC-37, which is not finalized. Read
[Token ACL](/docs/tokenization/token-acl) for the current state before you
commit to it, and account for the gate program's deployment, upgrade, audit, and
availability as dependencies distinct from your mint.

</Callout>

### When a hook is required

A [transfer hook](/docs/tokens/extensions/transfer-hook) fits rules that depend
on the transfer rather than on the holder. Lockups that expire per tranche,
jurisdiction-dependent limits on transfer size, and holder counts that must stay
under a statutory cap cannot be expressed as a list of permitted addresses.

The costs are compute on every transfer, a program to audit and maintain, and
reduced composability, since integrating venues need to account for the hook.
Where a list would express the rule, a list is the cheaper option.

### Allowlist or blocklist

Token ACL's gate is pluggable, so the same machinery expresses either. The
choice is a modeling decision about your default, not a mechanism.

An **allowlist** defaults to denied: nobody holds the token until they are
admitted. This is the fit for a supervised issuance, where every holder has been
onboarded and you can name them. It also fails safe - a gap in your onboarding
pipeline stops new holders rather than admitting unchecked ones.

A **blocklist** defaults to permitted: anyone holds the token unless excluded.
This suits a broadly circulating asset where the obligation is to exclude
specific addresses rather than to vet every holder. It fails open, so the list's
completeness and freshness determine whether it works.

Reach for a blocklist when the holder base is meant to be open and your duty is
exclusion.
[Gate the holders](/docs/institutional/tutorials/issuing/gate-the-holders)
covers both.

## Split authorities from the start

Token-2022 allows each power to sit on a different key. A mint where one key is
mint authority, pause authority, and permanent delegate concentrates the
consequences of a single compromise, and tends to attract attention in a
controls review.

| Authority              | What it can do                             | Suggested holder                                   |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Mint                   | Create new supply                          | Treasury quorum, offline                           |
| Metadata               | Change name, symbol, URI                   | Operations, low privilege                          |
| Pausable               | Halt all mint, burn, and transfer activity | Incident response, reachable 24/7                  |
| Permanent delegate     | Move or burn any holder's balance          | Legal and compliance quorum, offline               |
| Permissioned burn      | Co-sign redemptions                        | Redemption operations                              |
| Confidential balances  | Configure confidential transfer settings   | Same quorum as mint                                |
| Freeze (via Token ACL) | Freeze and thaw accounts                   | Delegated to the ACL program, with issuer override |

Two of these warrant particular attention. The **pause authority** needs to be
reachable quickly to be useful in the incident it exists for. The **permanent
delegate** can move a holder's assets, so a slower, well-witnessed process suits
it, and its uses should be individually evidenced.

This table says _who_ should hold each authority.
[Program and authority design](/docs/institutional/token-design/program-and-authority-design)
covers _what shape_ each holder should take - a program-enforced PDA, an onchain
multisig, or a single custodied key - along with rotation and the audit and
upgrade risk carried by every program in your transfer path.

## Controls you may also want

- **Pausable** - one action halts mint, burn, and transfer token-wide. This is
  the incident-response control.
- **Permanent delegate** - the forced-transfer and forced-burn power, for court
  orders, fraud, and lost-key recovery. See
  [permanent delegate](/docs/tokens/extensions/permanent-delegate).
- **Permissioned burn** - holders cannot burn unilaterally; you co-sign. See
  [permissioned burn](/docs/tokens/extensions/permissioned-burn).
- **Confidential balances** - balances and transfer amounts are hidden from the
  public ledger while remaining auditable by a designated auditor key. See
  [confidential transfers](/docs/tokens/extensions/confidential-transfer) and
  the privacy section of
  [risk and diligence](/docs/institutional/risk-and-diligence#privacy).
