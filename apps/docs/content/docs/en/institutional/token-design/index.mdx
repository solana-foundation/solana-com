<Callout type="info" title="Summary">

Decide first whether the token is the asset itself or a record of one held
elsewhere. That answer sets where your register of record lives and what you
have to reconcile. The asset then determines the control surface, and the
control surface determines your operational obligations. Settle all of this
before choosing extensions, because the ones enabled at mint creation are
difficult to change afterwards.

</Callout>

## Is the token the asset, or a record of one?

Answer this first. It determines where your register of record lives, and most
downstream obligations follow from it.

**The token is the asset.** The mint is the authoritative record of the
entitlement and no other register governs it. A stablecoin or e-money token is
the issuer's liability rather than a pointer to one held elsewhere. A tokenized
deposit, a natively issued note, or a fund whose share register is maintained
onchain work the same way.

**The token records an asset held elsewhere.** An off-chain register governs - a
custodian's books, a transfer agent's register, a position at a central
securities depository - and the token is a transferable record of a claim on it.
Tokenized equities commonly work this way, with shares held by a custodian and
the token tracking them.

Both models are in use. Neither is a stage on the way to the other: a program
may run the second model indefinitely, because the underlying asset has a
statutory register that cannot be relocated onchain.

|                      | Token is the asset                                            | Token records an asset held elsewhere                                      |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Register of record   | The mint                                                      | An off-chain register                                                      |
| Reconciliation       | Internal controls only                                        | A standing obligation between two books                                    |
| Main failure mode    | Loss or misuse of an authority key                            | Token supply drifting out of correspondence with the underlying            |
| The legal work       | Establishing that the chain record is the definitive register | Characterizing the token as a claim on the underlying                      |
| Why finality matters | It is the register                                            | It records a transfer of the claim                                         |
| Additional controls  | None inherent to the model                                    | Evidence of backing, and mint and burn tied to movements in the underlying |

The distinction cuts across asset types rather than following them: a fund or a
note can be issued either way. What settles it is where the register that a
court or a supervisor would look to actually sits.

<Callout type="warn">

In a mirror structure, tokens minted ahead of distribution are backed inventory
that no investor holds yet. Supply read straight off the mint will overstate
investor holdings, sometimes substantially. Decide early whether your published
figures report issued supply or investor-held supply, and label them, because
trackers commonly report issued supply, which a reader may take for investor
holdings.

</Callout>

## What is the same in both models

In either model the token is a record of an entitlement, and what that
entitlement is - a claim on a fund, a beneficial interest in a security, a
deposit liability, a bearer instrument - comes from the legal wrapper around it.
The chain enforces the transfer rules configured on the mint and holds no
representation of what the asset is.

Two tokens can also be technically identical and operationally very different. A
tokenized money market fund and a tokenized corporate bond may both need a
holder allowlist and a forced-transfer power, while only the fund needs a
mechanism for a daily NAV that changes every holder's balance without a
transfer.

## Match the asset to a control surface

| If the asset is                  | You will typically need                  | You may need                                                |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| A fund or money market fund      | Holder allowlist, forced transfer, pause | Scaled UI amount for NAV, permissioned burn for redemptions |
| A security or equity             | Holder allowlist, forced transfer, pause | Permissioned burn, metadata for corporate actions           |
| A deposit token or e-money claim | Holder allowlist, pause, forced transfer | Confidential balances, sponsored fees                       |
| A commodity or physical claim    | Holder allowlist, forced transfer        | Metadata pointing at custody attestations                   |
| A collateral instrument for repo | Forced transfer, pause                   | Atomic settlement against the cash leg                      |

The right-hand column is a common source of rework. Extensions that change how
balances are displayed or how supply is interpreted, notably
[scaled UI amount](/docs/tokens/extensions/scaled-ui-amount), can only be
enabled when the mint is created. Adding one later requires a new mint and a
migration.

A mirror structure adds to whichever row applies: mint and burn have to be tied
to movements in the underlying, and the correspondence between the two needs
evidence someone outside the issuer can check. Neither is a token extension, so
both live in your operating procedures rather than on the mint.

## Obligations follow from controls

Every control enabled on a mint is a power someone has to hold, exercise, log,
and justify. Enabling a control is a configuration change; operating it is a
standing obligation with named owners and evidence.

- **A holder allowlist** requires someone to maintain the list and to account
  for a wallet that should have been removed and was not. It also limits the
  token to venues that can hold an allowlisted position.
- **A forced-transfer power** (permanent delegate) means a key exists that can
  move any holder's balance. Document where it is held, who can use it, and what
  authorization evidence exists for each use.
- **A pause power** allows the token to be halted globally. Settle in advance
  who may invoke it, on what evidence, and how the unwind works.
- **Permissioned burn** requires the issuer to co-sign redemptions, which adds a
  liveness dependency on the issuer's own systems.
- **Backing evidence**, in a mirror structure, is an obligation the mint cannot
  carry for you. Someone has to attest that the underlying exists, on a cadence,
  in a form your reviewers are likely to accept.

Choosing fewer controls is a valid design. A token with no forced-transfer power
cannot satisfy a court order onchain, and equally cannot be drained through a
compromised delegate key. That trade-off belongs to the risk function.

## Decide these before you build

1. Is the mint the register of record, or does an off-chain register govern?
2. What does the token represent in law, and in which jurisdiction?
3. Who is permitted to hold it, and how is that determined?
4. Does the balance change without a transfer - accrual, NAV, splits?
5. What must you be able to do to a holder's balance without their cooperation?
6. Who signs for each of those powers, and what is the quorum?

Question 1 determines the answers to most of the others, and question 6 is often
deferred and among the hardest to change later. See
[choosing controls](/docs/institutional/token-design/choosing-controls#split-authorities-from-the-start).
