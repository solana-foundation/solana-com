    <div>
      <div className="poker-guide-overline">1/ The edge you already have</div>
      <h2>Why poker players make natural traders</h2>
    </div>
  </div>

<div className="poker-guide-lede">
  If you play poker seriously, you have already learned the hardest parts of
  trading. You know how to size a bet, calculate the odds, handle a downswing,
  and how to act on incomplete information. These habits that you have formed
  can be used as the basis for becoming a successful trader.
</div>
<div>
  Poker and trading are separate games with separate rules, but they ask for the
  same things from the participant. Both reward patience, arithmetic and
  discipline, and both punish the same mistakes.
</div>
<div>
  The vocabulary and the mechanics are what remain, and this guide covers both.
  It explains what a wallet is, how spot and perpetual futures (perps) trading
  work, how to judge an exchange, and how to keep your funds safe, with the
  poker equivalent alongside each idea where relevant.
</div>
<div>By the end of this chapter, you'll understand:</div>
<ul className="poker-guide-list">
  <li>
    Why the skills that make a strong poker player also make a strong trader
  </li>
  <li>
    What you need before you can place your first trade: a wallet, some SOL, and
    a stablecoin
  </li>
  <li>
    How spot trading and perps work, including longs, shorts, leverage and
    liquidation
  </li>
  <li>
    How to size positions and manage risk and a portfolio the way you manage a
    bankroll
  </li>
  <li>
    How to judge an exchange on Solana, and how to protect your funds once you
    hold them
  </li>
</ul>

<div className="poker-guide-art">
  <img
    src="/images/learn/poker-guide/poker-guide-habits.webp"
    alt="Five poker habits that apply to trading: expected value, bankroll management, incomplete information, variance, and emotional discipline"
  />
</div>

<h3>Five habits carry over directly</h3>
<ul className="poker-guide-list">
  <li>
    <strong>Expected value (EV):</strong> You already choose actions by their
    long-run payoff rather than their result on the day. A call that loses can
    still be correct, and a call that wins can still be wrong.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Bankroll management:</strong> You size your exposure to what you can
    afford to lose, and you never put the whole roll on a single hand. Traders
    call this position sizing and it does the same job.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Reading incomplete information:</strong> You act on partial
    information all the time, and you rarely see your opponent's cards. Markets
    work the same way. You will never have the full picture, and waiting for it
    means never acting.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Riding variance:</strong> You know the difference between running
    bad and playing badly. Markets produce long stretches where a sound approach
    still loses money, and the skill is telling the two apart.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Discipline over emotion:</strong> Tilt costs more money than bad
    cards. The same holds in trading, where chasing a loss or holding a losing
    position too long does more damage than any single decision.
  </li>
</ul>

</section>

<section id="first-trade" className="poker-guide-section">
  <div className="poker-guide-section-heading">
    <div>
      <div className="poker-guide-overline">2/ Before you trade</div>
      <h2>Get table-ready</h2>
    </div>
  </div>

<div className="poker-guide-lede">
  Three things sit between you and your first trade: a wallet, a small amount of
  SOL to cover network fees, and a stablecoin to trade with. None of it takes
  long, but each part is worth understanding before you move any money.
</div>
<div className="poker-guide-checklist">
  <div>
    <strong>01 / Wallet</strong>
    <div>The app that holds your funds and approves transactions.</div>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>02 / SOL</strong>
    <div>A small balance to pay for actions on the Solana network.</div>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>03 / Stablecoin</strong>
    <div>A dollar-denominated token to open and settle positions with.</div>
  </div>
</div>

<details className="poker-guide-details">
  <summary>A wallet: your keys, your approval</summary>
  <div>
    <div>
      A wallet is the app you use to hold your funds and approve transactions.
      It stores the digital keys that prove the funds are yours, and nothing
      moves without your approval. Nobody can access it without your approval,
      and no support desk can recover it if you lose access.
    </div>
    <div>
      Wallets come as browser extensions, mobile apps, and physical devices. Any
      of them will do for a first trade.{" "}
      <a href="/learn/what-is-a-wallet">What is a Wallet?</a> covers how they
      work in full, and you can compare your options at{" "}
      <a href="/wallets">solana.com/wallets</a>.
    </div>
    <div>
      When you set one up, you'll be given a seed phrase: a list of words that
      restores your wallet if you lose the device. Write it down, keep it
      offline, show it to nobody and store it securely. Anyone who has it can
      access your funds.
    </div>
  </div>
</details>

<details className="poker-guide-details">
  <summary>Getting funds into your wallet</summary>
  <div>
    <div>
      There are two common ways to fill a wallet and they suit different people.
    </div>
    <ul className="poker-guide-list">
      <li>
        <strong>Buy inside the wallet:</strong> Most wallets have a Buy button
        that lets you pay by card or bank transfer and receive crypto directly.
        It's the shortest route, and it works without an account anywhere else.
        Availability depends on your country.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Buy on an exchange, then withdraw:</strong> You open an account
        with a crypto exchange, verify your identity, buy what you want, and
        withdraw it to your wallet address. More steps, and it takes longer the
        first time, but it’s a common route for many traders.
      </li>
    </ul>
    <div>
      Some poker tournament operators now accept crypto payments directly, which
      is another way a poker player might already hold funds in the crypto
      ecosystem.
    </div>
    <div className="poker-guide-callout poker-guide-warning">
      <div>
        Whichever route you take, the same warning applies. When you withdraw
        from an exchange, you have to select the network you're sending on.
        Choose Solana. Sending Solana-based funds over a different network is
        one of the most common ways new users lose money, and the funds are
        usually unrecoverable. Send a small test amount first and confirm it
        arrives before you move the rest.
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</details>

  <details className="poker-guide-details">
    <summary>SOL for fees and a stablecoin to trade with</summary>
    <div>
      <div>
        Every action on Solana costs a small fee, paid in the native network
        token, SOL. This is true even when you're moving a different token, so
        you need a SOL balance regardless of what you plan to trade.
      </div>
      <div>
        The amounts are small. A typical transaction costs a fraction of a cent,
        so $2 of SOL can last a while. Some actions cost slightly more, and a
        little headroom is sensible, but you don't need to hold much. <a href="/learn/understanding-solana-transaction-fees">Understanding Solana Transaction Fees</a>{" "}
        covers this in detail.
      </div>
      <div>
        A stablecoin is a token designed to hold a fixed value, almost always
        one US dollar. The stable value is what separates it from SOL or any
        other token whose price moves.
      </div>
      <div>
        That makes it an ideal token to trade with as positions are usually
        opened, sized and settled in stablecoins, so your profit and loss reads
        in dollars rather than in a moving token.
      </div>
      <div>
        USDC is the main stablecoin on Solana. <a href="/learn/introduction-to-solana-tokens">Introduction to Solana Tokens</a>{" "}
        covers how it works.
      </div>
      <div>
        Stablecoins are not risk-free. They depend on the issuer holding real
        assets against every token, and that promise has broken before with
        other stablecoins. Use established ones, and understand that "stable"
        describes the intent rather than a guarantee.
      </div>
    </div>
  </details>
</section>

<section id="mechanics" className="poker-guide-section">
  <div className="poker-guide-section-heading">
    <div>
      <div className="poker-guide-overline">3/ The mechanics</div>
      <h2>Spot and perps</h2>
    </div>
  </div>

<div className="poker-guide-lede">
  There are two ways to trade on Solana. Spot trading, where you buy a token and
  own it. And perpetual futures, usually shortened to perps, where you take a
  position on a token's price without ever holding it. They work differently and
  they carry different risks, so it's important to understand the mechanics of
  both.
</div>

<h3>Spot trading</h3>
<div>
  Spot is the simple version. You exchange one token for another at the current
  price, and what you buy is yours to hold for as long as you like.
</div>
<ul className="poker-guide-list">
  <li>
    <strong>You own the asset:</strong> Buy $500 of SOL and you hold SOL. It
    sits in your wallet, and you can send it, hold it, or sell it whenever you
    choose.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Your risk is capped at what you paid:</strong> If the price halves,
    your $500 is worth $250. If the token goes to zero, you lose $500. You
    cannot lose more than you put in.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>There's no clock:</strong> Nobody closes your position for you. A
    trade you're down on is only a loss when you decide to sell it.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Profit comes from the price rising:</strong> You buy at $100, you
    sell at $130, you keep the $30 per token.
  </li>
</ul>
<div className="poker-guide-callout">
  <div>Spot is where most people should start.</div>
</div>

<h3>Perpetual futures (perps)</h3>
<div>
  A perp lets you take a position on a token's price without owning the token.
</div>
<div>
  The word perpetual means the position has no expiry date. It stays open until
  you close it, or until it's closed for you.
</div>
<div>Two directions are available:</div>
<div className="poker-guide-art">
  <img
    src="/images/learn/poker-guide/poker-guide-long-short.webp"
    alt="A long position profits when price rises and a short position profits when price falls"
  />
</div>
<div className="poker-guide-compare">
  <div>
    <h3>Long</h3>
    <div>
      You profit when the price goes up. Open a long on SOL at $150, and if it
      rises to $165, you're up.
    </div>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Short</h3>
    <div>
      You profit when the price goes down. Open a short on SOL at $150, and if
      it falls to $135, you're up. Spot gives you no way to do this; it is a
      perp-only mechanic.
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<h3>Leverage and collateral</h3>
<div>
  Perps let you take a position larger than the money you put up; this is called
  leverage. The money you put up is your collateral, also called margin. It's
  held against the position for as long as the position is open, and it's what
  you stand to lose.
</div>
<div>
  Say you post $1,000 of collateral and open a 10x leverage long. Your position
  is $10,000, ten times the money behind it. Every 1% move in the price now
  moves your position by $100, which is 10% of your collateral.
</div>
<div>It works in both directions:</div>
<ul className="poker-guide-list">
  <li>
    <strong>The price rises 5%:</strong> Your $10,000 position gains $500.
    Against $1,000 of collateral, you're up 50%.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>The price falls 5%:</strong> Your position loses $500. You're down
    50%, and half your collateral is gone.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>The price falls 10%:</strong> Your position loses $1,000. That's all
    of your collateral, and the position is closed, or liquidated.
  </li>
</ul>

<h3>Liquidation</h3>
<div>
  When your losses reach the collateral committed to the position, the exchange
  closes it automatically and that collateral is gone.
</div>
<div>
  The leverage figure tells you how far the price can move against you before
  that happens. At 10x, roughly 10%. At 20x, roughly 5%. At 50x, roughly 2%.
  Daily moves of that size are not unusual in crypto. These figures assume the
  position is backed only by the collateral you posted; holding more collateral
  than the position requires moves the liquidation point further away.
</div>
<div>Two mechanics are worth being clear on:</div>
<ul className="poker-guide-list">
  <li>
    <strong>Liquidation is automatic:</strong> Once the price hits the
    liquidation level, the position closes. There's no margin call to respond to
    and no pause to add collateral at that point.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>A liquidated position doesn't recover:</strong> If the price dips
    12% and then rises 40%, the call was right, but you were liquidated on the
    dip and are no longer in the trade.
  </li>
</ul>

  <h3>Risk</h3>
  <div>
    Leverage does not change the odds of your call being right. It changes the
    size of the outcome in both directions, and it sets a price level at which
    the position closes whether you want it to or not. A spot holder facing the
    same 12% dip still owns the token.
  </div>
  <div>
    Never risk more than you can afford to lose. Start on spot, and if you move
    to perps, start at low leverage.
  </div>
</section>

<section id="poker-player" className="poker-guide-section">
  <div className="poker-guide-section-heading">
    <div>
      <div className="poker-guide-overline">4/ The habits transfer</div>
      <h2>Trade like a poker player</h2>
    </div>
  </div>

<div className="poker-guide-lede">
  While the mechanics above are new, as a poker player you already know what to
  do with them. Everything that follows is probably a habit you already have,
  just applied to a different game.
</div>

<h3>Sizing positions against a bankroll</h3>
<div>
  You already know not to sit down with your whole bankroll. A cautious player
  wants 100+ buy-ins for the cash game or tournament they're about to enter; a
  more aggressive one might be comfortable with 50. The number varies with risk
  tolerance, but the principle doesn't: no single session should threaten your
  ability to keep playing.
</div>
<div>Trading works the same way, and the math transfers directly.</div>
<ul className="poker-guide-list">
  <li>
    <strong>Decide what a single position can cost you:</strong> A trader
    risking 1% of a $10,000 portfolio has $100 at stake on any one trade. That's
    the equivalent of choosing a stake level relative to your roll.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Work backwards to the position size:</strong> If you'd close the
    trade after a 10% adverse move, then $100 of risk means a $1,000 position.
    The exit level and the risk you'll accept together determine the size.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Count leverage as part of the exposure:</strong> A $1,000 position
    at 5x leverage is $5,000 of exposure. That larger figure is the one to size
    against.
  </li>
</ul>
<div className="poker-guide-callout poker-guide-warning">
  <div>
    The mistake looks familiar. A player who moves up stakes after a losing
    night has abandoned bankroll management, and so has a trader who doubles
    their position size to make back yesterday's loss.
  </div>
</div>

<h3>Set an exit before you enter</h3>
<div>
  Position sizing only works if you know where you'll get out, which means
  deciding before you open the trade. Two order types do this for you.
</div>
<div>
  A stop loss closes your position automatically if the price falls to a level
  you set. A take profit closes it automatically if the price rises to a level
  you set. Both are placed when you open the trade.
</div>
<ul className="poker-guide-list">
  <li>
    <strong>Set the stop loss when you open the position:</strong> Deciding in
    advance means deciding while you're calm.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Place it where the trade idea is invalidated:</strong> If a price
    move to $140 means your reasoning was mistaken, the stop loss belongs at
    $140, whatever the dollar figure attached to it.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Set a take profit as well:</strong> Decide what the trade is worth
    if it works, and let the order close it there. A winning position with no
    exit plan is one you'll close on instinct, either too early out of nerves or
    too late out of greed.
  </li>
</ul>

<h3>Time horizon and variance</h3>
<div>
  Variance matters less over a longer sample. One 200-hand session tells you
  very little; 50,000 hands offers a better signal. A strong player with a small
  edge can lose for months and still be a winner over the years.
</div>
<div>
  Markets behave the same way. A price that swings 15% in a week may be up 100%
  over a year, and the trader holding across that period is exposed to far less
  noise than trading it hour by hour or day by day.
</div>
<div>
  Your edge is also harder to measure here. In poker you can watch the table and
  see who's playing badly. In a market you can't see the other players at all.
  Trading over longer horizons reduces how much that matters, because your
  result depends less on out-trading anyone and more on whether your read on the
  asset was right.
</div>
<div>The practical version:</div>
<ul className="poker-guide-list">
  <li>
    <strong>Fewer decisions means fewer chances for a bad one:</strong> A longer
    holding period cuts your exposure to short-term noise.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Short-term trading is a tougher game:</strong> The faster you trade,
    the more you're up against firms with better data, better speed and
    full-time focus. That's a reason to be honest about your edge before you
    size up.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Take shots deliberately:</strong> Treat a shorter-horizon trade the
    way you'd treat a shot at a bigger game: a small, fixed portion of the roll,
    decided in advance, with a clear point at which you go back down.
  </li>
</ul>

  <h3>Discipline over emotion</h3>
  <div>Tilt in trading looks much the same as tilt at the poker table.</div>
  <ul className="poker-guide-list">
    <li>
      <strong>Chasing losses:</strong> Opening a new position immediately after
      a bad one, sized larger, to get back to even. The market doesn't know
      you're down and doesn't owe you a recovery.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Refusing to close a loser:</strong> You wouldn't commit more chips
      to a pot you know you're losing. Don't hold a losing trade because you
      don't want to realize the loss.
    </li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section id="venue" className="poker-guide-section">
  <div className="poker-guide-section-heading">
    <div>
      <div className="poker-guide-overline">5/ Choose where to trade</div>
      <h2>Read the venue</h2>
    </div>
  </div>

<div className="poker-guide-lede">
  Solana has a number of places to trade. Some handle spot only, some offer
  perps, and they vary in cost, available liquidity, and in how long they have
  been running.
</div>

<h3>Centralized and decentralized exchanges</h3>
<div>
  There are two types of exchanges, and the difference is in who holds your
  funds while you trade.
</div>
<div>
  A centralized exchange, or CEX, takes custody of your funds, much like a
  typical online poker site. You open an account, verify your identity, and
  deposit funds with them. They hold the funds and match your trades internally.
  If you bought crypto on an exchange and withdrew it to your wallet, you have
  already used one.
</div>
<div>
  A decentralized exchange, or DEX, mostly runs onchain and never takes custody
  of your funds. Some DEXs let you trade straight from your wallet. Others,
  particularly for perps, ask you to move collateral into a trading account
  first. Either way you keep sole control of the funds, and nobody else can move
  them or withhold them from you.
</div>
<div>Both spot and perps are available on DEXs.</div>

<h3>Types of DEXs</h3>
<div>
  DEXs work in one of two ways, and the difference is in how your order gets
  filled.
</div>
<div className="poker-guide-art">
  <img
    src="/images/learn/poker-guide/poker-guide-markets.webp"
    alt="An automated market maker prices a trade against a pool of tokens while a central limit order book matches orders from other traders"
  />
</div>
<div>
  An automated market maker, or AMM, prices your trade against a pool of tokens
  rather than against another trader. The pool always has a price and it always
  takes the other side, so your trade fills immediately. The price you get
  depends on the size of your order relative to the size of the pool. A large
  order against a small pool moves the price as it fills, and you end up paying
  more than the price you were quoted. This is slippage.
</div>
<div>
  A central limit order book, or CLOB, matches your order against orders placed
  by other traders. This is the same model used by traditional exchanges. You
  can place an order at a price you choose and wait for someone to take it, or
  take an existing order at the price on offer. A trade only happens when both
  sides agree on a price.
</div>
<div>
  On Solana, spot trading is mostly done through AMMs, and perps mostly through
  order books.
</div>

  <h3>What to look for</h3>
  <ul className="poker-guide-list">
    <li>
      <strong>Liquidity:</strong> How much is being traded, which determines
      whether your order fills near the price you saw. In a thin market the
      price moves against you as you buy, and again when you sell. Volumes are
      published, though they can be inflated, so treat a big number as a
      starting point rather than proof.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Fees:</strong> Rates differ between exchanges, and the difference
      compounds if you trade often. The fee schedule is usually published on the
      exchange's website, alongside documentation on its mechanics and failure
      cases.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Spot or perps:</strong> Some exchanges offer one, some both. The
      risks differ, so know which you are using.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Track record:</strong> How long the exchange has operated, how
      much has been traded through it, and how it performed in volatile
      conditions. An exchange that kept working through a sharp drawdown has
      been tested in a way a newer one hasn't.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Verify the URL:</strong> Fake versions of well-known exchanges
      exist to drain the wallets that connect to them. Bookmark the real one
      and use the bookmark.
    </li>
  </ul>
  <div className="poker-guide-callout">
    <div>
      Start small. A first trade with a small amount confirms the exchange works
      as expected, fills, fees and withdrawals included, before you put real
      size through it.
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section id="protect" className="poker-guide-section">
  <div className="poker-guide-section-heading">
    <div>
      <div className="poker-guide-overline">6/ Protect your bankroll</div>
      <h2>Keep the keys</h2>
    </div>
  </div>

<div className="poker-guide-lede">
  A trading wallet connects to exchanges and signs transactions, so it's more
  exposed than a wallet you only hold funds in. A few habits keep that in check.
  <a href="/learn/staying-safe-on-solana">Staying Safe on Solana</a> covers
  general wallet security, including how to store your seed phrase and the
  warning signs of a scam.
</div>

<h3>Signing transactions</h3>
<div>
  Every action onchain requires you to sign a transaction in your wallet, and on
  Solana one signature can move everything in the wallet at once. Fake exchanges
  are built around this: the site copies a real one, the wallet connection
  screen behaves normally, and the transaction you're asked to sign doesn't look
  like a transfer.
</div>
<ul className="poker-guide-list">
  <li>
    <strong>Reach exchanges through your own bookmark:</strong> Search results
    and ads regularly lead to fake versions of sites people have used before.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Read what you're signing:</strong> Your wallet shows what a
    transaction does. If it moves funds when you only expected to connect,
    reject it.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Slow down when you're being hurried:</strong> Countdown timers,
    limited spots and expiring offers are there to stop you checking.
  </li>
</ul>

<details className="poker-guide-details">
  <summary>Tokens you didn't buy</summary>
  <div>
    <div>
      Unexpected tokens turn up in wallets. They're advertising: the name or
      description points at a website, and the website is where a loss can
      happen. The token itself can't do anything while it sits there.
    </div>
    <ul className="poker-guide-list">
      <li>
        <strong>Don't visit the site it points to:</strong> That's what the
        token is for.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Don't try to sell it:</strong> Many are built so they can't be
        sold, and some let the creator burn or seize them at will.
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Hide it:</strong> Most wallets let you hide tokens from view.
      </li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</details>

<h3>Check how the supply is held</h3>
<div>
  Token ownership is public. If a small number of wallets hold most of the
  supply, they can sell into you and control the price action. Check this before
  initiating the trade.
</div>

  <div>
    Use more than one wallet. A trading wallet holds what you're actively
    trading and connects to exchanges. A second wallet holds longer-term
    positions and connects to nothing. If the trading wallet is compromised,
    the loss is limited to what was in it.
  </div>
  <div className="poker-guide-callout poker-guide-warning">
    <div>
      Only trade what you can afford to lose. Money you need for something else
      has no place in a trading position.
    </div>
