<ZineCopy eyebrow="Class notes · 01" title="A summer spent learning by shipping">

Solana Summer School brought college students together for a focused path from first principles to finished projects. The format was simple on purpose: **five weeks of live classes**, followed by **four weeks of capstone building** and a final demo day. Students could learn the network’s architecture, practice with the tools used by Solana developers, and then turn those lessons into something they could show.

The cohort was global from the beginning. **1,164 students applied from 69 countries**, joining with different levels of experience and a shared interest in understanding how applications are built on Solana. Some arrived already comfortable with Rust. Others were writing their first onchain program. The course gave both groups the same destination: a working project and a clearer sense of what to learn next.

</ZineCopy>

<StudentProjects />

<ZineCopy eyebrow="Build log · 02" title="The capstone changed the shape of the course" accent="yellow">

The weekly lessons were not designed to end as notes in a folder. Each topic added another piece students could use in a real application: account models, program structure, token mechanics, client generation, and live data. During the capstone phase, office hours replaced the lecture cadence and the work became more individual. Teams had to choose a problem, narrow the scope, connect the pieces, and decide what was important enough to ship.

By demo day, **20 teams presented**. The project wall in the zine captures the range of interfaces and experiments that emerged from the cohort. More important than any single screenshot was the transition behind it: students moved from following an exercise to making product and engineering decisions of their own.

</ZineCopy>

<ZineCopy eyebrow="Field report · 03" title="Global reach, small enough loops" accent="purple">

The numbers tell two stories at once. The applicant map shows the reach of a remote program built for students across time zones. The class and capstone schedule shows how that large community was broken into practical learning loops: learn a concept, try it, bring questions to office hours, and return with a better implementation.

- **1,164 applicants** entered the program funnel.
- **69 countries** were represented across the applicant pool.
- **Five weeks** were dedicated to live technical classes.
- **Four weeks** gave teams room to build capstones with support.
- **20 teams** made it to the demo-day presentation.

Those figures matter because developer education is ultimately measured in momentum. A lesson becomes useful when a student can use it to unblock the next decision, explain the tradeoff to a teammate, or turn an idea into code that runs.

</ZineCopy>

<SummerSchoolCurriculum />

<ZineCopy eyebrow="Syllabus · 04" title="From Solana’s model to real-time applications">

**Week one** established the foundation: Solana architecture, transactions, program-derived addresses, Rust, and Anchor. Students put those concepts to work immediately with their first vault and escrow programs.

**Week two** moved into Token Extensions and Token-2022, including transfer hooks and rate limits. The challenge was to combine those primitives into a stablecoin implementation rather than study them in isolation.

In **week three**, the class stepped below Anchor into Pinocchio and native Rust. Zero-copy patterns and compute-unit optimization made performance concrete: students could see how design decisions change what a program costs to execute.

**Week four** widened the stack through Metaplex and Codama. Students explored NFT and compressed NFT standards, custom candy machines, and generating TypeScript clients from interface definition language files.

**Week five** connected programs to the world outside a transaction. Anchor events, Geyser and Yellowstone gRPC streams, and real-time listeners introduced the indexing patterns applications use to stay current.

**Weeks six through nine** belonged to the projects. The calendar shifted to focused office hours so teams could debug, simplify, and finish with direct support.

</ZineCopy>

<SummerSchoolLecturers />

<ZineCopy eyebrow="Office hours · 05" title="Lessons from people building the stack" accent="yellow">

Guest sessions connected the syllabus to work happening across the ecosystem. The lecturers brought perspectives from developer relations, technical education, AI, protocol engineering, and the tooling that helps new developers move faster. Their role was not only to explain how something works, but to show why particular abstractions, performance choices, and learning paths matter in practice.

The lecture roster moved between code, product, developer relations, AI, and protocol engineering. Their sessions gave the cohort direct context for the abstractions and tradeoffs in the syllabus.

</ZineCopy>

<ZineCopy eyebrow="Margin notes · 06" title="Confidence was one of the things students built" accent="purple">

The clearest measure of the program came from the students themselves. Their notes describe technical progress, but they also describe a change in what felt possible: writing a first Anchor program, shipping a complete dApp, contributing to the ecosystem, or considering a global career for the first time.

</ZineCopy>

<ZineCopy eyebrow="Next semester · 07" title="The end of summer is another starting point">

The cohort ended with demos, but the learning path continues. The next opportunities highlighted in the zine are the **Solana School fall class** and the **University Ambassador Program**. Both carry the same idea forward: make it easier for students to find structured learning, meet other builders, and create a first contribution that leads to a second.

Students can keep building through the [Solana developer resources](https://solana.com/developers), revisit the concepts behind their capstones, and stay connected to the people they met during the course. The strongest outcome of a summer school is not a finished syllabus. It is a community that knows how to keep going.
