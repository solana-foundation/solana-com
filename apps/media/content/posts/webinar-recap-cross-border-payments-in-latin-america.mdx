**Cross-border payments in Latin America are moving beyond stablecoins as back-end settlement rails.** Payment firms are increasingly using them for treasury, reserves, remittances, savings, and yield products—while seeking to reduce the capital tied up in prefunded local accounts.

In a recent webinar, Jorge Borges, Head of Latin America at Fireblocks, and Antonio Neto of the Solana Foundation discussed how firms across the region are putting stablecoin payments into production.

## Why stablecoins gained traction in Latin America

Correspondent banking was not designed for a 24/7 digital economy. Settlement can take one to five days, intermediary costs can reach 5% to 7% in many corridors, and prefunding can leave working capital sitting in local accounts.

Regional demand also created conditions for adoption. Argentines hold dollars to protect savings against inflation, Colombians commonly maintain dollar reserves, and many people use two or three fintech apps—giving new providers an opportunity to compete without displacing a single incumbent banking relationship.

Neto described a pattern in which consumer usage precedes formal regulation: people adopt first, companies follow, and rules catch up. Brazil followed that path before formalizing a virtual asset service provider license.

## The cost of prefunding

Neto compared settlement delays to a supply chain: when a buyer in Brazil pays a supplier in Asia, the supplier cannot ship until the transfer clears. For remittance companies, the same delay leaves capital idle across correspondent accounts.

Western Union had achieved real-time cross-border settlement before adopting stablecoins, according to Borges, but still faced the cost of maintaining float and collateral. He described the potential capital impact as follows:

> “So why is Western Union starting using stablecoins? Because it costs $1.5 billion in pre-funding and other kinds of collateral that they have to post to make this happen. So they can free up this capital by using stablecoins.”
>
> — Jorge Borges, Head of Latin America at Fireblocks

Before Western Union adopted stablecoins, a SWIFT-routed transfer could reach 8% of a transaction, while the company maintained correspondent accounts around the world to ensure local currency was available for payouts.

## From settlement rails to onchain treasuries

Early implementations used stablecoins as plumbing between two fiat endpoints. A Brazilian real deposit could be converted into dollar stablecoins, sent to a processor in the destination country, then paid out in local currency.

Companies are now moving treasury and reserves onchain, where they can access money markets and tokenized funds without leaving the network. Solana settles transfers in under a second at a fraction of a cent. In February 2026, Solana recorded [roughly $650 billion in stablecoin transaction volume](https://www.theblock.co/news/markets/2026-03-04-solana-stablecoin-volume-record-650-billion-february-onchain-payments-demand-grayscale-392217), the highest monthly figure on any network that month.

## LATAM payment products in production

Firms across the region are applying stablecoins to consumer accounts, remittances, treasury operations, and digital-asset services.

* [Wenia](https://www.wenia.com/es), part of Bancolombia Group, began with buy and sell services, added a Colombian peso stablecoin, and now offers yield products.
* [Nubank](https://nubank.com.br/), which has more than 100 million customers, pays a return on dollar stablecoin balances without a separate signup process.
* [Bitso](https://bitso.com/) grew from zero to close to 10% of volume in the US-to-Mexico corridor, which the speakers described as the world’s largest retail remittance corridor.
* [ARQ, formerly DolarApp](https://www.dolarapp.com/), offers a global account with faster access and a tighter spread, while running part of its treasury on stablecoins that customers do not see.
* [BTG Pactual](https://www.btgpactual.com/) has operated in digital assets for more than 10 years, beginning with custody.

Borges said demand is shifting across corridors, with volume into Mexico declining while Caribbean corridors grow. He described the commercial opportunity in terms of becoming the main app a customer uses to move money: cross-border payments can open an account, while payments, savings, yield, and trading can deepen the relationship.

## What remittance companies need to get started

Stablecoins are permissionless, but companies need operational and compliance infrastructure before they can support payments across corridors. Neto identified four requirements:

* **Wallet infrastructure** to connect to the network and scale from one wallet to many.
* **On- and off-ramp partners in each corridor.** A company in Panama paying out in Brazil needs a Brazilian partner or must become one.
* **Liquidity** through either a provider relationship or a direct mint account with an issuer.
* **AML and KYT screening** for transaction monitoring and policy controls from day one.

These components can support several entry paths, from back-end treasury operations to launching a stablecoin.
