# **Why This Matters, by Stakeholder**

* **Users and dapp developers.** Lower average handover latency means less idle time between leaders, and thus more slots per unit of wall-clock time. This may affect transaction senders, who should not assume that consecutive leaders are geographically independent.
* **Validators.** Validators in sparse locations benefit most, as they are much more likely to have a nearby predecessor than today. The number of leader windows a validator gets in an epoch is unchanged, only their respective order changes.
* **Core contributors.** The leader schedule computation becomes more complex and depends on reported locations.

# TL;DR

* Geo-aware leader schedule is promising to improve handoff latency, reducing pressure for stake concentration in macroregions
* This analysis shows that the `STAKE_FLOOR` parameter (denoted with $\sigma$ here) should stay around 10%
  * Higher values make the fake-location attack more expensive, but above 10%, Asia (about 10.4% of stake today) loses local bins
* For the `RUN_LENGTH` parameter (denoted with $\gamma$ here) should not go above 7
  * It depends on which risk we want to minimize, and ranges between 3 and 7
* When coalitions are considered, if we fix $\sigma=10\\%$, our simulation framework suggests a limited absolute increase in MEV-opportunity rising from consecutive leader windows

# Introduction

Solana’s leader schedule is what decides which validators are entitled to produce a slot.

The current schedule assigns windows of 4 consecutive slots to each validator every epoch. The selection criteria are based on validators’ stake.

Despite being costly to attack, a random schedule carries a well-known issue: it ignores where validators are located. Two consecutive leaders can sit in the same data center or on opposite sides of the planet. In the second case, the handoff (passage from one leader to the other) costs tens of milliseconds of pure propagation delay.

[SIMD-0675](https://github.com/solana-foundation/solana-improvement-documents/pull/675) proposes a geo-aware leader schedule. In order to preserve some of the good properties of the random scheduler, the core extraction mechanism is not replaced. It takes the same random schedule as input and only changes the order. Every validator keeps exactly the same number of leader windows it would have had.

The reordering depends on two parameters (see Fig. 1):

* **$\sigma$ (**`STAKE_FLOOR`**):** For every location we compute a radius $r(\sigma)$: the smallest distance that contains at least a fraction $\sigma$ of the total stake. Dense areas get small radii, isolated validators get large ones. Two leaders are compatible when their distance is within the larger of their two radii
* **$\gamma$ (**`RUN_LENGTH`**):** We go through the random schedule in order. Each leader joins a compatible open bin, chosen at random, or opens a new one. When a bin reaches $\gamma$ leaders, it is shuffled and written to the final schedule as one contiguous block.

<Figure src="/uploads/posts/geo-aware-leader-schedule/01d_radius_schedule_mechanics.png" alt="geo-aware schedule mechanism">
  **Fig. 1:** Left: the founder radius at \sigma=10\\% and the locations compatible with it. Centre: compatible leaders accumulate in the bin until \gamma=10. Right: the full bin is shuffled and emitted contiguously.
</Figure>

The idea is simple: consecutive leaders become geographically close, so the handoff becomes shorter. But this comes at a price: the final ordering is moving away from the random leader schedule.

The goal of this analysis is to quantify the trade-off between latency gain and attack surface expansion, eventually providing a guide for parameter selection. Precisely, we try to answer the question: How much latency do we gain for a given choice of $(\sigma,\gamma)$, and how far does the schedule move from random to get it?

The analysis is structured to first present all the metrics employed. In the second part we use the observed behaviour to provide sensible values for these parameters.

Relevant SIMDs are

* [**SIMD-0674: Validator Location Registration**](https://github.com/solana-foundation/solana-improvement-documents/pull/674)
* [**SIMD-0675: Geo-Aware Leader Schedule**](https://github.com/solana-foundation/solana-improvement-documents/pull/675)

# The Parameter Selection Tradeoffs

Network state is constantly changing. Stake can redistribute, nodes can move around, new coalitions can form. Thus, we can’t study how parameters affect today’s network, but we need to add perturbations.

In this analysis, we keep the 124 locations where Solana validators run today, grouped in six macroregions, and generate 6,250 synthetic networks on top of them by redistributing nodes and stake with different levels of concentration (see Appendix A).

Due to the absence of evidence of other sites ready to host Solana nodes today, we add no new locations to the simulation pipeline. The geography therefore stays as unbalanced as it is now.

All conclusions are conditional on this geography. If new locations become available in the smaller regions, or stake moves significantly across regions, they should be revisited.

## Handoff Gain

In order to measure the latency improvement, we employ the handoff $H$ for a schedule $S$ defined as the average latency between consecutive leaders,

<Latex formula="H(S)=\frac{1}{W-1}\sum_{t=1}^{W-1}l_{L_t,L_{t+1}}," />

where $W$ is the total number of leader windows in the simulations (108,000 here), $L\_t$ is the leader of window $t$, and $l\_\{i,j}$ is the estimated latency between leader $i$ and leader $j$. We estimate it from distance only: $l\_\{i,j}=1.5\\,d\_\{i,j}/(200\ \text\{km/ms})$, i.e. signals travel in fiber at about two thirds of the speed of light along a path 50% longer than the great-circle distance.

The gain over random is

<Latex formula="\Delta H = H(S_\text{random})-H(S_\text{radius})," />

so positive values mean the geo-aware schedule is faster than random.

<Figure src="/uploads/posts/geo-aware-leader-schedule/00_unconstrained_handoff_gain_full.png" alt="Mean handoff gain across all synthetic networks in the simulations">
  **Fig. 2:** Mean handoff gain across all synthetic networks in the simulations, for every parameter selection.
</Figure>

Figure 2 shows two clear trends. The gain grows with $\gamma$ and shrinks with $\sigma$. At $\sigma=10\\%$, it goes from 15.8 ms at $\gamma=2$ to 33.9 ms at $\gamma=50$. At fixed $\gamma$, moving from $\sigma=2\\%$ to $\sigma=20\\%$ costs roughly a third of the gain.

This behaviour is expected by the nature of the parameters:

* A smaller $\sigma$ means smaller radii, so leaders in the same bin are closer to each other
* In a block of $\gamma$ leaders, $\gamma-1$ handoffs are between compatible leaders and only the last one jumps to the next bin. The share of long jumps is then roughly $1/\gamma$.

It is interesting to observe that, the second point explains why the gain saturates. Going from $\gamma$ to $\gamma+1$ only removes a small share of the long jumps, and that share keeps shrinking. At $\sigma=10\\%$, $\gamma=3$ already gives 65% of the gain observed at $\gamma=50$, $\gamma=5$ gives 79%, and $\gamma=10$ gives 91%. Beyond $\gamma\approx10$, each additional leader per bin buys very little latency.

## Geographic Persistence

The purpose of a geo-aware schedule is to group leader from the same area. Inevitably, this means that the network stays longer in one macroregion. If that region has a problem, such as an outage at a large provider, local censorship or a regional network partition, the network is exposed to it for longer than under random.

This is clearly lowering the resilience of the network. Thus, we need to measure the “loss” we get when we change the parameters.

We measure the probability that $k$ consecutive leader windows all fall in the same macroregion,

<Latex formula="p_k^G(S)=P(G(L_t)=\cdots=G(L_{t+k-1}))\,," />

where G(L) is the macroregion of the leader L. We measure excess cumulative geographic persistence relative to random as

<Latex formula="D_G=\sum_{k=1}^{W}\left[p_k^G(S_\text{radius})-p_k^G(S_\text{random})\right]." />

For each $k$, the starting position is uniform over the $W-k+1$ valid positions. $D\_G$ sums excess homogeneus-block probabilities across lengths. It measures cumulative geographical persistence.

It is worth mentioning that, $D\_G$ also provides a proxy for additional time spent consecutively in the same macroregion. For $k\ll W$, and for suffciently larger $W$, the residual same-region duration can be expressed as

<Latex formula="\Delta T_{res}\sim \tau D_G\,," />

where \tau=0.8s represents the duration of one leader window. Consequently, D\_G is expressed in leader windows, and multiplying it by 0.8s gives a time-scaled persistence measure. Throughout the text and figures, references to additional windows or seconds of geographic persistence use this approximate interpretation.

<Figure src="/uploads/posts/geo-aware-leader-schedule/02b_macroregion_persistence_distance_full.png" alt="mean excess persistence as a function of the window length">
  **Fig. 3:** Left: mean excess persistence p\_k^\{radius}-p\_k^\{random} as a function of the window length k (in leader windows). Right: D\_G, MC mean with 5th–95th percentiles across synthetic networks.
</Figure>

The excess peaks at short horizons, a few windows, and then decays. Two things control it.

* It is clear that $\gamma$ is the dominant factor\*\*.\*\* Larger bins mean longer same-region blocks, so both the peak and the tail grow. With $\gamma=2$ the excess is gone after about 20 windows; with $\gamma=50$ it lasts for a few hundred
* Moving $\sigma$ reduces the excess of geographic persistence\*\*.\*\* This is because a larger $\sigma$ gives larger radii, so bins mix more locations and more regions. At $\gamma=50$, $D\_G$ goes from about 61 windows at $\sigma=2\\%$ to about 23 at $\sigma=20\\%$, and the spread across networks shrinks as well.

This is the first trade-off. For the handoff, smaller $\sigma$ and larger $\gamma$ are better. For persistence, both are worse. Larger bins buy latency with longer stays in one region, and smaller radii make those stays more likely.

## Regional Occupancy

Persistence counts uninterrupted runs. A single leader from another region breaks a run, even if the network then goes back to the same region. To capture how concentrated the schedule is over a whole interval, we look at the share of the most represented macroregion in a window of $\ell$ consecutive leader windows

<Latex formula="M_\ell(t)=\max_r \frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{u=t}^{t+\ell-1}\mathbf{1}\left\{G_u=r\right\}\,." />

Under random, every interval contains each region roughly in proportion to its stake. A curve shifted to the right of random means that intervals are more often dominated by one region.

We use four horizons: 38, 375, 1,125 and 2,250 leader windows. With 200 ms slots these are about 30 seconds, 5 minutes, 15 minutes and 30 minutes.

<Figure src="/uploads/posts/geo-aware-leader-schedule/10_rolling_region_occupancy_cdf_full.png" alt="CDF of the largest regional share over rolling intervals">
  **Fig. 4:** CDF of the largest regional share M\_\ell over rolling intervals of \ell leader windows, for random (dashed) and selected (\sigma,\gamma).
</Figure>

The main message of Fig. 4 is that concentration is a short-horizon effect.

* At 38 windows (\~30 s) the differences are large. With $\gamma=2$ the schedule is indistinguishable from random. With $\gamma=10$ the curves move clearly to the right. With $\gamma=50$ a large fraction of intervals is dominated by a single region, for every $\sigma$. At $\sigma=10\\%$, the mean excess of $M\_\ell$ over random is 0.5 percentage points at $\gamma=3$, 5.9 at $\gamma=10$ and 29.5 at $\gamma=50$.
* At 2,250 windows (\~30 min) all configurations mostly overlap with random.

This follows from how the schedule is built. The leader multiset is preserved and bins are flushed roughly in the order they fill. A leader is moved only by the time it takes its bin to fill. Over long horizons the regional composition is therefore the same as random; what changes is how regions are arranged inside the interval. Instead of being mixed, they alternate in blocks.

<Figure src="/uploads/posts/geo-aware-leader-schedule/10c_fixed_sigma_rolling_region_occupancy_30_seconds_full.png" alt="Regional share over 38 leader windows by macroregion.">
  **Fig. 5:** Regional share over 38 leader windows at \sigma=10\\%, by macroregion.
</Figure>

Figure 5 shows who is affected over short horizons. In Europe and North America, larger $\gamma$ makes intervals more extreme: with $\gamma=50$ about a quarter of the 38-window intervals contain no European leader, and roughly the same share contains only European leaders. Asia changes the most in relative terms. Under random, only about 7% of these intervals have no Asian leader; this rises to about 40% at $\gamma=10$ and above 70% at $\gamma=50$. Africa, Oceania and South America barely move. Their leaders mostly end up in bins founded in other regions (see the spillover section), so they are spread out rather than grouped.

The trade-off is the same as for persistence, but sharper. Short-horizon concentration grows quickly with $\gamma$, while most of the latency gain is already reached at small $\gamma$.

## Cross-Region Spillover

The radius is defined by stake, not by geography. If a macroregion holds less than a fraction $\sigma$ of the total stake, the radius of its locations has to reach outside the region to collect enough stake. Its bins then mix local and foreign leaders.

For bins founded in macroregion $r$, we measure the share of slots that belong to leaders from other regions,

<Latex formula="X_r=\frac{\text{external slot in $r$-founded bins}}{\text{total slots in $r$-founded bins}}\,." />

The founder is always local, so $X\_r$ cannot exceed $1-1/\gamma$.

Spillover is not a risk with respect to random: a random block mixes regions even more. It tells us instead where the geo-aware schedule actually works. A region with high spillover gets little of the latency gain, and its leaders are spread inside other regions' bins.

<Figure src="/uploads/posts/geo-aware-leader-schedule/05_actual_bin_spillover_full.png" alt="External slot share in bins founded in each macroregion">
  **Fig. 6:** External slot share in bins founded in each macroregion, against the ratio between regional stake S\_r and \sigma. Each point is one region in one synthetic network. Dashed line: S\_r=\sigma.
</Figure>

Figure 6 shows that one quantity explains spillover: the ratio $S\_r/\sigma$. All regions collapse onto the same curve.

* When **$S\_r>\sigma$**, bins are almost self-contained. Spillover stays mostly below 10% and decreases as the ratio grows.
* When **$S\_r\<\sigma$**, spillover rises steeply and quickly reaches its mechanical maximum. Bins founded in the region are filled almost entirely by foreign leaders.

The transition is sharp and sits at $S\_r=\sigma$. This is the reason for an upper bound on $\sigma$: any region with less stake than $\sigma$ is effectively excluded from local bins.
