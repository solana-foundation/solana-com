## Nebius and the AI infrastructure shift

The architecture of the internet is changing as automated systems account for a growing share of online activity. In May, Cloudflare measurements showed [machine-generated web traffic surpassing human-generated traffic](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/jun/6/internet-traffic-ai-agents-bots-surpasses-people/?srsltid=AfmBOorG2Jp25ud5wIcmIA_T096fCPM1_c3_27Fv9gIP7mlmeLLRr74v) for the first time, while present-day Cloudflare Radar data shows [automated traffic continuing to account for the majority of HTTP requests to HTML content](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic#bot-vs-human).

Cloudflare has projected that, if current trends continue, human-generated traffic could eventually become a [“rounding error”](https://www.theregister.com/networks/2026/08/07/humans-will-be-a-rounding-error-on-the-internet-says-cloudflare-exec/5284429) relative to machine activity — a trend compared in the draft to the [internet adoption metrics that inspired Jeff Bezos to leave D.E. Shaw](https://a.co/d/06KK4kPX) and pursue Amazon.

Nebius Group (Nasdaq: NBIS) operates what has become known as a neocloud: a cloud computing platform purpose-built around the demanding requirements of artificial intelligence. Where traditional hyperscalers such as AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud were built as general-purpose computing platforms retooled for AI, Nebius has constructed its stack around AI from the ground up.

Nebius designs infrastructure and server systems, operates large GPU clusters, builds its own cloud software, and increasingly offers higher-level services for developers moving AI applications from training into production. By controlling both the raw GPU hardware layer and the agent middleware, Nebius is positioning itself as an integrated platform for startups building multi-agent systems that need live internet access across many services.

## What Nebius builds

Nebius wraps GPU clusters optimized for training and inference in a software layer for tuning, deployment, and production serving. The company is attacking cost-per-agent-action across the layers an agent touches.

* **Context pruning:** Through its Tavily acquisition, Nebius aims to strip noisy data into clean, structured, model-ready context, cutting input tokens by up to 80%.
* **Inference:** Token Factory supports high-throughput hosting of open-weight models like DeepSeek and Qwen, with prompt caching and dynamic batching to reduce the marginal cost of calls inside agentic loops.
* **Serverless orchestration:** Agentic work can be asynchronous and bursty, requiring on-demand serverless compute that matches capacity to activity instead of paying for peak capacity around the clock.
* **Physical GPU infrastructure:** Beneath the software layer, Nebius is building a GPU backbone for asynchronous, always-on agents.

## From tokens to tasks

For much of the generative AI era, the industry focused on cost per token: a user submits a prompt, the model processes tokens, and the model returns a response. Autonomous agents behave differently.

One request to an agent can trigger a chain of activity: searching the web, retrieving documents, reasoning over them, calling tools, inspecting results, searching again, executing an action, verifying the action, retrying after an error, and continuing through the task.

For agentic software, a more useful economic framework may become cost per agent action — or, ultimately, cost per successfully completed task. Nebius identifies cost predictability as a central problem for production agent systems, noting that poorly planned execution paths can multiply inference spending; its [Agents Blueprint](https://dev.nebius.com/blueprints?_gl=1*jrl18p*_gcl_au*MTA4MjM2MTcyNi4xNzg2MjQzMjIw) combines inference, orchestration, retrieval, grounding, observability, and evaluation into a shared architecture for running agents reliably and economically at scale.

The challenge becomes less about producing one intelligent answer and more about making millions or billions of autonomous actions economically viable.

## Jevons Paradox and AI demand

In the 19th century, [economist William Stanley Jevons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jevons_paradox) observed that improvements in the efficiency of coal use did not necessarily reduce coal consumption. By making coal more economically useful, efficiency helped expand the number of applications that consumed it.

AI infrastructure could follow a similar trajectory. Lower inference costs do not necessarily mean lower aggregate demand for compute; they can make new workloads economically viable at scale.

An agent that costs $10 to perform a task might only make sense for high-value enterprise work. Reduce that cost dramatically, and the same architecture can begin answering support tickets, researching user data, monitoring systems, managing automated workflows, coordinating tasks, booking services, operating machines, and interacting with thousands of other agents.

In the agentic era, compute alone is unlikely to determine the economics of AI infrastructure. Throughput, tool access, orchestration, and the cost of completing useful work matter too — and if Jevons Paradox holds, falling costs could expand aggregate demand for compute, requiring enormous amounts of physical infrastructure and the capital to build it.
