_Part 4/5 in the[ @SolanaEvents](https://x.com/@SolanaEvents) guide to thriving (and surviving) at Breakpoint London 2026_

You're in London, you know how to get to Olympia, and hopefully you've had a nosh beyond an airport sandwich. Maybe even a full English. Now for the main event.

Breakpoint is where the timeline converges IRL. Your magic internet friends have faces, the projects have people behind them, and that person you've wanted to meet for months might be standing behind you in the coffee queue. Making the most of it takes a little preparation, some social awareness, and enough energy to still be yourself by the final day.

Being the loudest person in the room is optional. Preferably not.

## Plan the Week, Stay Dynamic

Pull up the priority list from Article 1. Co-founder, collaborators, work, ideas, or finally hanging out with friends: whatever brought you here should guide your week.

Make those goals specific. "Meet people" can mean anything. "Talk to three teams that might need my design skills" gives you somewhere to start.

## Use Your Calendar!

People: Who would you regret leaving London without meeting? Reach out ahead of time with who you are, why you're interested, and a possible time. Don't just say "hey bro" and wait for them to ignore you. And don't be wishy-washy when someone asks to set a time with you, either.

Sessions: Know the stage and start time for the talks you need to see live, and protect the time to get there.

Side events: Browse the Breakpoint community calendar and BlastCtrl's side events list. Apply early and track approvals. An application doesn't mean you're in.

Breathing room: Leave gaps for travel, food, queues, and conversations that run over.

A useful meeting request might look like this:

"hey, i'm maurice (@mauricedotxyz). i'm compiling practical guides for solana event attendees and would love your perspective given your role in the ecosystem. could we sync for 15 minutes after your panel? if not, happy to work around your sched!"

A specific reason beats "Would love to pick your brain."

## Make Logistics Easy for Others

Set your calendar to London time.

Pick a real meeting point: a named booth, an entrance, a cafe with an address. "At Olympia" is a location, not a meeting point.

Keep updates in one chat. Telegram is the practical default for this crowd.

Cancel promptly if plans change. If someone no-shows, send one considerate check-in and carry on. Don't spam.

Check the Breakpoint website and London FAQs each morning. Don't build your week around an old screenshot.

## Sessions Matter as Much as the Space Between Them

Going to the most sessions doesn't mean getting the most out of Breakpoint. Booths, demos, community activations, and hallway chats are the core of the experience.

If someone you've been trying to connect with finally has ten minutes, choose deliberately. Talks are all recorded and posted to X and YouTube afterward. That person may not have another opening. Equally, if a session directly serves your goal, go. There's no prize for skipping every talk, and you may meet like-minded people in the same room.

Leave space for something outside your usual corner of Solana. Terminally in the trenches? Ask a payments team how its product works. There can be alpha in the most unassuming conversations.

Plan enough to have direction. Leave enough space for something better to happen.

## Show Up Ready

Dress like yourself, with some consideration for why you're there. You don't need a suit or a Patagonia vest, but first impressions count when you're repping a company or looking for work. Comfy shoes and the raincoat beat an outfit you can't move in.

## Make That Person-to-PFP Connection Easy

If you have a card or badge with your handle and PFP, wear it visibly. Introduce yourself with your name and handle, especially if they're completely different. Don't make people solve your identity like some cryptographic puzzle.

When you reconnect, give context: "We spoke in the MonkeDAO Discord voice chat last month." Nobody enjoys "You know who I am, right?" Keep your profile bio and links useful. Yes, you can keep the pixelated PFP!

## Only Carry What You'll Use

Keep the bag manageable. A fanny pack or crossbody beats a heavy backpack. Bring the laptop only when you need it, not out of habit.

Keep essentials accessible. Phone, power bank, cable, ticket.

Handle the basics. Breath mints and deodorant belong in the plan. If you wear fragrance, go light; the person beside you didn't opt into your cologne.

## Phone + Links

Put your Telegram or X profile QR on your lock screen. Hope we don't need to say this but: use a public profile link, never a login or authentication code. Have your portfolio link ready to open rather than searching your own account while someone waits.

Save tickets, the venue map, and your hotel address somewhere you can reach without data. Need step-free routes or other support? Contact the organizers beforehand.

## Merch Selectively Because It's Best to Travel Lite

Take the shirt if you want the shirt. Just remember every tote and hoodie is something you're carrying while trying to shake hands. Free stops feeling free when it becomes an extra baggage problem.

If collecting is part of the fun, do it early in the week, plan a hotel drop-off, and leave something for the next person.

Representing a brand? Stickers with a clear name, handle, or QR double as business cards, and one on a laptop keeps the introduction alive.

## Human Powered

Good small talk gives the other person somewhere to take the conversation. "What are you working on?" "What brought you here?" "What's your favorite thing you've seen today?" Even "Where did you fly in from?" works. The opening line matters less than what you do with the answer. People want to talk about themselves, remember this!

Ask, then actually listen. Active participation is different than waiting to respond. If someone mentions hiring or a launch, ask about that instead of reciting your bio. Don't inflate your role or fake understanding. People will see through it.

## First Breakpoint?! This Is Where You Belong

You don't need a big following or an OG badge. Plenty of people are also working out where to stand.

Introduce yourself to someone standing alone: "Hey, my name is ______. What brought you to this year's Breakpoint?!"

Join an open group with a smile and a nod rather than waiting for a perfect opening or loudly announcing you're inserting yourself into the convo.

Let a friend introduce you, then give the new conversation room. Don't spend the whole trip inside your existing group.

And if you're the person who knows everyone, make an introduction. "You both work on developer onboarding" gives them more to use than "You should meet."

## Read the Rooms

Seeing someone you've wanted to meet is exciting. It doesn't automatically make their current conversation less important than yours.

A casual group may welcome another person; a focused one-on-one probably needs a moment. Wait for a natural pause or offer a quick "Hey, would love to catch up when you're free." Then give them room. If someone is checking the time or giving short answers, offer an easy exit: "Do you need to head somewhere?" Don't take it personally.

You want to be remembered for the connection, not the interruption.

## Short Pitches Invite Questions!

Have a plain-English version of who you are, what you're doing, and why it's relevant to this person. Start with the problem and who you help, not your entire stack:

"We help small teams handle stablecoin payouts without managing every transfer manually. We're looking for partners and operators willing to test it."

That's enough for someone to decide whether to ask more. Save the longer version for when they show interest.

Be specific about what you want. "We need feedback on the onboarding flow" gives someone a way to help. "We're open to synergies" mostly gives them a way to leave. And think about what you can offer back.

## It's OK to Walk Away While the Convo Is Still Hot

You don't have to keep talking until the interaction sucks all the air out of the room:

"Great meeting you. Let's exchange details and pick this up next week."

A clear goodbye is kinder than pretending to listen while scanning the room for someone else.

## Know What You're Asking For

You don't need a different personality for every interaction. You do need to help people understand what you're looking for and whether they can help.

| Here for | Lead with | Ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Work | Your skill, the work you want, and something you've actually built | How the team hires, or who the right contact is |
| Collaborators / co-founder | The problem, the work already done, and the gap you're filling | Another conversation to compare skills and expectations |
| Clients / partners | Questions about their current process and pain points | Something small: feedback, a trial, a call |
| Investors | The problem, users, progress, and what's live vs. planned, in 1–2 sentences | Whether it fits their interests and how they prefer to continue |

"I built this wallet onboarding flow and I'm looking for a product team" beats "I'm looking for opportunities." Repping a team? Don't promise terms that need someone else's approval. And a polite "I'll take a look" isn't a funding commitment.

Here to learn or just vibe with frens?! That's a complete, valid, and common reason to attend. Not every coffee needs to become a BD meeting.

A first conversation doesn't need to close the deal. Often, its job is to earn the next conversation.

## The Conference Floor: Booths, Demos, Speakers

### Let Them Demo

At a booth, let the team show what they've built, and be mindful of people waiting behind you. Get beyond "Looks cool":

- Who is this for, and what problem does it solve?
- Is this on mainnet, devnet, or simulated?
- What's the biggest friction point for users right now?

If an answer gets too technical, ask for an example. Selling something to the project? Ask who handles that rather than pitching in a busy demo queue. And someone eating lunch isn't on duty just because you recognize their shirt.

### Give Speakers Some Room to Breathe

Let speakers finish, don't corner them on the way backstage, and keep your first question concise. A Q&A microphone is not free ad inventory for your startup. Ask one clear question, then stop. During sessions, silence notifications and take calls outside.

### Staffing a Booth?

Agree who covers demos, partnerships, and follow-up, and keep a backup recording in case the live demo fails. A handle exchange isn't an invitation to every channel you operate.

## ABC: Always Be Collecting Contacts (With Context)

When you both use Telegram, send a quick message while you're still together so the chat exists:

"hey, it's maurice (@mauricedotxyz). good meeting you at the demo booth. i'll send over the link we discussed."

Now you have a name, a place, and a reason to reconnect. Check the actual username, not just a familiar PFP, and use a Telegram folder or private note to keep conference chats organized.

### The Good Ole Selfie Trick

A selfie helps connect a face to an account later. Just make sure the conversation matters more than collecting proof that it happened.

One rule covers a lot here: ask first. Before the selfie, and separately before posting or tagging it on socials. Before filming, quoting, or sharing someone's offhand comment. Before forwarding their details, starting a group chat, or making an intro. And before a hug. Respect a no without making it awkward.

## After Dark + Energy

### Check Luma

Most side events in the Solana space use Luma. Confirm your status, the address, entry requirements, and any arrival deadline. Don't assume a Breakpoint badge gets you into a separately organized event.

If you're waitlisted, be considerate to door staff and use your backup plan. "Don't you know me?" is never an acceptable entrance fee. Can't make something you booked? Release the spot.

Don't turn every evening into a venue speedrun. Stay long enough to have real conversations.

### Enjoy the Parties But Don't Become the Story

If you drink, stay aware, respectful, and in control. People will remember how you behaved, especially if you're representing a company or looking for work. All jokes aside: blacking out could spell disaster for your career. And not drinking is normal; nobody needs an explanation.

One more pint isn't automatically one more useful connection. Sometimes the best move for tomorrow is going home tonight.

### How Will You Get Home? Don't Decide at 3% Battery

Check your route before you leave, and if you book a car, confirm the vehicle and driver match. Arrived with friends? Agree who's leaving together.

### Manage Your Energy (Mental + Physical)

Long days on your feet, constant conversation, and late nights wear you down. Five or ten quiet minutes can reset you; take longer if you need it.

Eat before you're desperate. Have a fallback for dietary needs.

Bring your own meds, including usual over-the-counter essentials. Don't count on the hotel or info desk having painkillers.

Protect some sleep. Work back from tomorrow's first commitment.

Tell a friend if you're struggling. "I need ten minutes somewhere quiet" is a complete explanation.

## Stay Open, Stay Secure

Article 2 covered wallet hygiene. On the floor, it still applies even when the demo is polished and the presenter is friendly:

Know what you're signing. If you don't understand a connect, sign, or approve request, decline.

Verify links. Logos and QR codes aren't proof. Confirm domains and follow-ups through official channels.

Limit exposure. Use a separate demo wallet, keep balances private, and never share recovery phrases or private keys.

Keep your phone yours. Let people scan your QR from your screen; never hand over an unlocked phone. Watch your drink and bag, and if something feels wrong, go to event staff.

You can be open to a new connection and still take time to check what they're asking of you.

## Before the Day Ends

Note who you want to speak to again and anything you promised. Send promised links now if you can.

Check tomorrow's first commitment, charge everything, and repack.

Ask whether today changed your priorities, then update the plan. That's what makes it dynamic.
