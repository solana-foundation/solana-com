# Content modes

Choose the mode by what the reader needs from the content, not merely by where
the string happens to be stored. A single page may use more than one mode: a
developer landing page can contain a builder-oriented hero, factual product
cards, and a reported update feed.

## Main-site marketing, product, and solution pages

Give visitors a clear reason to care, then show the relevant capability or
proof. The tone is confident and energetic, but never grandiose. Lead with the
outcome a real audience can achieve; explain the mechanism or differentiator;
name the next action.

Keep a section focused on one message. Use a memorable heading only if its
supporting copy makes the promise concrete. Do not mistake brand energy for
claims about inevitability, industry leadership, or a universal future.

## Developer hub and developer landing pages

Treat a developer landing page as an orientation layer, not as reference
documentation or a news article. Its job is to help a builder decide where to
begin and move into the right resource quickly.

- Use a confident, technically literate, builder-first tone. Speak in real tasks
  and choices: learn the model, start from working code, migrate an existing
  app, test locally, or read the relevant guide.
- Give each section one navigational job. A heading states the path or outcome;
  the supporting line identifies the capability or resource; the CTA names the
  destination.
- Make the hierarchy easy to scan. Prefer short, purposeful headings and terse
  supporting copy over a long explanation of the platform.
- Explain terminology only where it helps a newcomer choose a path. Let the
  destination documentation provide full technical depth.
- Do not describe the page's curation, the “developer journey,” or the site's
  internal organization. “Build a program, start from code, or migrate an
  existing app” is useful orientation; “a complete ecosystem for every stage” is
  not.

This mode is practical rather than promotional. The page should make a capable
builder feel oriented and ready to act, not sold to.

## Core developer documentation and API references

Optimize for correctness, successful completion, and fast retrieval. Be literal,
ordered, and technically precise. State prerequisites, inputs, commands,
expected results, limitations, and failure modes where relevant. Avoid marketing
adjectives, motivational framing, and claims of simplicity unless the source
provides a concrete comparison or constraint.

## Tutorials, cookbook entries, templates, and learning material

Teach by doing. Start with the task, say what the reader will build or change,
then guide them through the smallest useful path. Use welcoming language without
talking down to the reader. Templates and catalog cards should help people make
a selection: identify the starting point, the use case, and any meaningful
prerequisite or technology.

## News, changelogs, releases, and upgrade explainers

Report the fact first. Name the actor, the change, and the affected system; then
explain the practical consequence only when it is supported. Attribute quotes
and distinguish completed work, proposals, and future plans. Technical upgrade
content may need more explanation than a changelog, but its confidence should
still come from mechanisms, timelines, and sources—not hype.

## Features, case studies, podcasts, and event recaps

Use a reported narrative. Open on the person, team, product, or event fact that
gives the story its reason to exist. Supply context before interpretation,
attribute viewpoints, and use examples, metrics, or quotes to carry the story
forward. Preserve a named author's point of view when editing an existing draft.

## Research and data-led content

Be sober and exact. Define the scope, measure, time period, and source before
drawing a conclusion. Separate data from interpretation, retain material
caveats, and avoid turning a trend into a prediction.

## Events and community pages

Invite participation with real energy anchored in specifics: what is happening,
who it is for, where and when it occurs, and what a visitor can do next. Strong
event language is welcome when it reflects the program, speakers, or experience
on offer. Avoid stock excitement, vague superlatives, and promises that the
agenda or event details do not support.

## Shared UI, metadata, and system messages

Match the mode of the rendered surface, not the package or catalog that stores
the string. Keep labels, empty states, validation, accessibility text, and
metadata especially concise and literal. Metadata should summarize the
page-specific value; it should not repeat a generic brand slogan.

## Quick decision check

Before finalizing, ask whether the copy would still make sense if it appeared on
a different kind of page. If it would not, that may be correct—voice should
serve the page's job. If it would fit everywhere, it probably needs a more
specific audience, task, fact, or outcome.
